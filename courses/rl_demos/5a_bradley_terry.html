The Bradley-Terry Model

Optional aside · read any time

Chapters 5, 6 and 14 all use −log sigmoid(a − b) and none of them says where it came from. This is where. Nothing here is required — but it is the one piece of statistics underneath all three losses.

Turning "A Beat B" Into Numbers

The setup, and where you have already met it

You have a pile of pairwise results and nothing else — no scores, no ratings, just who won:

A beat B C beat A B beat D A beat D ...

You want one number per item, so you can rank them and predict future matchups. That single problem shows up in all of these:

FieldItemsThe number you want
Chessplayersa rating (Elo)
LLM leaderboards (LMArena)modelsthe arena score
Ch. 5 reward modelresponses to a promptRM(prompt, response)
Ch. 6 DPOresponses to a promptβ·log(P_cur/P_ref)

Why the obvious answer — win rate — is broken

Just count wins over games played. Now look at two players who both went undefeated:

playerrecordwin rate
Alicebeat the reigning champion, once1/1 = 100%
Bobbeat a beginner, nine times9/9 = 100%

Win rate calls them equal. Obviously they are not. Win rate has no idea who you played. It throws away the single most informative thing in the data — the strength of your opponent.

Note this is not a contrived flaw. It is exactly the situation in a preference dataset: a response that beat a strong response has told you much more than one that beat a weak response, and "fraction of comparisons won" cannot see the difference.

What we want instead, stated as a wish

A number per item — call it that item’s strength — such that the strengths explain the results we saw. Concretely, we want to be able to say:

"Given these two strengths, the probability that this one wins is ___"

Then "which strengths best explain the data?" becomes a well-posed question with an answer. That is Bradley-Terry: one number per item, plus a rule for turning two numbers into a win probability. The next tab builds the rule.

The Rule, Built From Scratch

Step 1 — give each item a strength, and let it be any real number

s(A) = 1.0 s(B) = 0.0 s(C) = −2.0
 
// Unbounded on purpose. Negative is fine. There is no natural
// zero and no natural scale — we will see why on tab 3.

Step 2 — the naive attempt, and why it fails

We need a probability out of two strengths. Simplest idea: share the total in proportion to strength.

P(A beats B) = s(A) / ( s(A) + s(B) ) // ?
 
// Try it with our numbers:
P(A beats C) = 1.0 / ( 1.0 + (−2.0) ) = 1.0 / (−1.0) = −1.0
 
// A probability of MINUS ONE. Broken: strengths can be negative
// or sum to zero, and proportions of negatives are nonsense.

Step 3 — fix it the way chapter 3 already fixed it: exponentiate first

This is the same move as the softmax in ch. 3. To turn unbounded real numbers into positive ones, apply exp, then take proportions:

P(i beats j) = exp(s_i) / ( exp(s_i) + exp(s_j) ) // Bradley-Terry
 
// exp of anything is positive, so the ratio is always in (0,1). ✓
 
// Check the broken case from step 2:
exp(1.0) = 2.718 exp(−2.0) = 0.135
P(A beats C) = 2.718 / (2.718 + 0.135) = 2.718 / 2.853 = 0.953 ✓

A is much stronger than C, so a 95% win probability is exactly the sort of answer we wanted.

Step 4 — and now the identity: that formula IS the sigmoid of the gap

This is the step chapters 5 and 6 quietly assume. Do the algebra — divide top and bottom by exp(s_i):

exp(s_i)
───────────────────────
exp(s_i) + exp(s_j)
 
// divide numerator and denominator by exp(s_i):
 
1 1
= ───────────────────── = ────────────────────
1 + exp(s_j)/exp(s_i) 1 + exp(s_j − s_i)
 
// and exp(s_j − s_i) = exp( −(s_i − s_j) ), so:
 
1
= ──────────────────────── = sigmoid( s_i − s_j )
1 + exp( −(s_i − s_j) )

Two consequences, and both are facts chapters 5 and 6 state without proof.

  • Only the difference matters. The formula depends on s_i − s_j and nothing else. Add 100 to every strength and every predicted probability is identical. That is why ch. 5 says "add a constant to every RM score and the loss is unchanged."
  • Sigmoid is not a design choice here — it is what "share the total in proportion to exp(strength)" equals. It arrives as a consequence, not a decision.

Read the middle column: gap 0 means a coin flip — "no opinion." That is the same 0.5 that ch. 6 tab 2.1 calls the starting belief, and it is why an untrained model sits there.

Deeper: where does exp really come from? (the noisy-performance story)

Step 3 justified exp as "a trick to make things positive," which is honest but shallow. There is a real story underneath.

Suppose each item has a fixed strength but performs with random daily variation, and the better performance on the day wins:

performance_i = s_i + noise_i
i wins <=> performance_i > performance_j

If that noise follows the Gumbel distribution, then working out P(performance_i > performance_j) gives back exactly exp(s_i)/(exp(s_i)+exp(s_j)). Bradley-Terry is precisely the statement "fixed strengths plus Gumbel noise, highest performance wins."

This is the same mathematics that makes softmax the right choice for classification, and the same fact behind the "Gumbel-max trick" for sampling. If you use Normal noise instead you get a slightly different curve (that model is called Thurstone / probit) — it behaves almost identically but has no closed form, which is why Bradley-Terry is the one everybody uses.

Where −log sigmoid(gap) Actually Comes From

Step 1 — the question that defines the loss

We have a rule with unknown strengths, and a pile of observed results. The question:

"Which strengths make the results we actually saw the MOST LIKELY?"

That is maximum likelihood, and it is the entire derivation. Everything below is bookkeeping on that one sentence.

Step 2 — the probability of one observation

We observed "w beat l". Under the model, the probability of seeing that is just the rule from tab 1:

P( we see "w beat l" ) = sigmoid( s_w − s_l )

Step 3 — the probability of ALL observations, and why logs appear

// Assume the comparisons are independent, so multiply:
Likelihood = sigmoid(gap₁) × sigmoid(gap₂) × ... × sigmoid(gap_N)
 
// A product of N numbers below 1 underflows fast — with 100k
// comparisons this is astronomically small. Same problem, and same
// fix, as ch. 6 Step 0: take the log, and the product becomes a sum.
 
log Likelihood = log sigmoid(gap₁) + log sigmoid(gap₂) + ...

Step 4 — flip the sign, because optimizers minimize

// "Maximize log-likelihood" = "minimize NEGATIVE log-likelihood":
 
Loss = − Σ log sigmoid( s_w − s_l )
 
// and for a single pair, that is:
Loss = −log sigmoid( s_w − s_l )

That is the formula. Chapter 5’s reward-model loss, chapter 6’s DPO loss and chapter 14’s SimPO loss are all this single line with different things substituted for s:

Wherewhat plays the role of s(response)
Ch. 5, reward modelRM(prompt, response) — a separate network's output
Ch. 6, DPOβ · log( P_cur / P_ref ) — computed from the LLM itself
Ch. 14, SimPO(β/|y|) · log P_cur — length-normalized, no reference

Nothing else differs. Every one of those chapters is Bradley-Terry with a different definition of strength.

The loss 0.693 at gap 0 is worth memorizing — it is log 2, the "I know nothing" loss, and it is the number your training curve should start at.

Three Built-In Pathologies (You Have Seen All Three Already)

1. The scale is arbitrary — only differences exist

Tab 1 showed the model depends only on s_i − s_j. So the fit has no unique answer:

s = (A: 1.0, B: 0.0, C: −2.0)
s = (A: 101.0, B: 100.0, C: 98.0) // +100 to everything
 
// Both give IDENTICAL predictions and IDENTICAL loss.

Where you met it: ch. 5’s reward model outputs raw unbounded numbers and the page warns "don’t read 0.82 as a probability — only the gap means anything." This is why. In practice people pin the scale by convention (Elo fixes an average; some RM code standardizes the scores).

2. An undefeated item has INFINITE strength — the "no stop gate"

Suppose A has won every comparison it appears in. What strength best explains that? Push the gap up and watch the loss:

s(A)gap vs B (s=0)P(A wins)loss
110.7310.313
330.9530.049
550.9930.0067
10100.999950.000045
→ ∞→ ∞→ 1→ 0, never reaching it

Maximum likelihood wants s(A) = +∞. There is no finite best answer, because sigmoid never actually reaches 1, so the loss can always be reduced a little further by pushing harder.

This is the deep reason behind something you already met twice. Ch. 5 tab 4’s "the RM loop has no natural stop" and ch. 6’s "the first loss never stops working" are not quirks of those implementations — they are this property of Bradley-Terry itself. Every fix in those chapters (early stopping at ~1 epoch, validation-accuracy gating, SimPO’s target margin γ, IPO’s bounded loss) exists to supply the stopping rule that maximum likelihood refuses to provide.

3. Contradictory data freezes — and that sets the accuracy ceiling

Humans disagree, so real data contains cycles:

A beat B B beat C C beat A
 
// No ranking can satisfy all three. What does the fit do?
// By symmetry, every item wins once and loses once, so the pulls
// cancel exactly and all three strengths stay EQUAL:
 
s(A) = s(B) = s(C) → every gap = 0 → every P = 0.5
loss stuck at −log(0.5) = 0.693 on these pairs, forever

Where you met it: ch. 5 tab 7 derives the reward model’s 65–75% accuracy ceiling from exactly this. Cyclic pairs are unlearnable, not under-trained — more epochs cannot resolve a contradiction, they can only memorize which contradictory pairs happened to appear, which is the definition of overfitting.

4. And one that only shows up in leaderboards: disconnected groups

A beat B (many times) C beat D (many times)
 
// Nobody from {A,B} ever played anybody from {C,D}.
// You can slide the whole second group up or down by any amount
// without changing the loss at all. A-vs-C is UNDETERMINED.

Win rate would happily report "A: 100%, C: 100%, therefore tied." Bradley-Terry instead tells you the honest answer: that comparison is not identified by your data. This is why arena leaderboards need every model to play a shared pool of opponents, and why a preference dataset wants overlapping prompts rather than isolated islands.

The Same Model in Its Other Clothes

Elo is Bradley-Terry with a rescaled axis

Chess ratings look nothing like sigmoid(s_i − s_j), but they are the same model:

// Elo's expected-score formula:
P(i beats j) = 1 / ( 1 + 10^((R_j − R_i)/400) )
 
// Bradley-Terry:
P(i beats j) = 1 / ( 1 + e^(s_j − s_i) )
 
// Identical, with one change of units. Since 10^x = e^(x·ln10):
s = R × ln(10) / 400 = R / 173.7

So Elo points are just Bradley-Terry strength with a friendlier scale. The famous landmark falls straight out:

Elo gapstrength gapP(stronger wins)odds
000.5001 : 1
2001.1510.760~3 : 1
4002.3030.90910 : 1

"400 points = ten times as likely to win" is the standard chess rule of thumb, and it is exactly ln(10) = 2.303 in strength units. Elo also differs in how it fits: instead of solving for all strengths at once, it nudges after every game — online gradient ascent on the same likelihood, with the K-factor as the learning rate.

LLM arenas: the same fit, on models instead of players

// Items = the models
// Comparison = a human picks the better of two anonymous replies
// Fit = Bradley-Terry over all those votes
// Output = the leaderboard number

Which means the leaderboard inherits every pathology from tab 3: the scale is conventional (differences are the only real content), a model that has played few opponents has a poorly-determined score, and human disagreement caps how sharply models can be separated at all.

Review questions

QuestionOne-line answer
"What is the Bradley-Terry model?"One latent strength per item, with P(i beats j) = exp(s_i)/(exp(s_i)+exp(s_j)), fitted by maximum likelihood over observed pairwise comparisons.
"Why does the reward-model loss look like −log sigmoid(gap)?"It is the negative log-likelihood of that model: the sigmoid is what the exp-share formula equals, and the log turns the product over comparisons into a sum.
"Why are RM scores unbounded and only meaningful as differences?"The model depends only on s_i − s_j, so the scale and origin are unidentifiable — adding a constant to every score changes nothing.
"Why does RM training overfit so fast?"Maximum likelihood drives an undefeated item's strength to infinity, so the loss never stops shrinking — the stopping rule has to come from outside (validation accuracy, ~1 epoch).
"Why can't a reward model exceed ~70% accuracy?"Human disagreement creates cyclic comparisons whose gradients cancel exactly; those pairs are unlearnable at any amount of training.
"How is Elo related?"Same model, rescaled: s = R·ln(10)/400, fitted online one game at a time with K as the learning rate.
"Why not just use win rate?"Win rate ignores opponent strength, so beating a champion once and a beginner nine times look identical.

Recap — the whole chapter in five lines

#Step
1Give every item one strength, any real number.
2exp them so they are positive, then take proportions → exp(s_i)/(exp(s_i)+exp(s_j)).
3Divide through and that equals sigmoid(s_i − s_j) — so only gaps matter and sigmoid was never a choice.
4Ask which strengths make the observed results most likely; take the log of the product, flip the sign → −log sigmoid(gap).
5Ch. 5, 6 and 14 are that one line with RM(·), β·log(P_cur/P_ref) and (β/|y|)·log P substituted for strength.
