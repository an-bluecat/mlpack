2026-08-23 New course — SVD, built from one 2×2 matrix you can do with a pen

Muon ch. 7 leans on singular values throughout, and its "refresher" foldable was carrying more weight than a foldable should. This is the proper treatment: four chapters, one running example M = [3 0; 4 5], every number hand-computable and confirmed by a run. It starts from "what does a matrix DO" and never quotes a formula before deriving it.

Start here

Ch. 1 in order — it is the whole idea in three tabs and needs nothing but "a matrix times a vector." Ch. 2 if you want to compute one; ch. 3 if you want to read one; ch. 4 for the payoff.

whatchange
newSVD ch. 1 — Circles into ellipsesNo factorization at all. Opens with a trap: both columns of M have length 5, so you would guess it "stretches by about 5" — but sweeping the unit circle finds 6.7082 at 45° and 2.2361 at −45°, and neither extreme is a coordinate axis. That gap is why SVD exists: a matrix's entries are how it is stored, not what it does. Then push the whole circle through and an ellipse comes out (SVG, real polyline data) — always, with a one-paragraph reason why linearity forbids an egg or a dent. The ellipse needs four numbers; so does the matrix; same information, better language. Ends by separating the three objects people conflate: v (input directions), u (output directions, the ellipse's axes), σ (the stretch) — tied by M vᵢ = σᵢ uᵢ, verified both ways.
newSVD ch. 2 — Where U, S and V come fromM = USVᵀ is derived, not announced: write the two Mvᵢ = σᵢuᵢ equations, stack them into columns to get MV = US, then use V⁻¹ = Vᵀ (because the v's are perpendicular unit vectors — checked on the numbers). Then the question ch. 1 dodged: where do σ and v come from? Maximize ‖Mx‖² over unit x, expand it, and MᵀM falls out with no cleverness — the door between geometry and arithmetic. Three properties of MᵀM with one-line proofs each explain why the v's are guaranteed perpendicular and the σ's guaranteed real. Then the full hand computation: MᵀM = [[25,20],[20,25]] → (25−λ)²=400 → λ = 45, 5 → σ = 3√5, √5 → v = [1,±1]/√2 → u = Mv/σ = [1,3]/√10 and [3,−1]/√10 → reassemble, get M back. Foldable on why numpy prints flipped signs (paired sign flips leave M unchanged — an ellipse axis has no arrow).
newSVD ch. 3 — Rank, condition number, energy, compressionFour readings of the same list of numbers. Rank = count of nonzero σ (a zero σ means a crushed direction; an outer product has rank 1 — the Muon fact). κ = σ₁/σₙ = how far from circular, which is exactly the optimizer course's ill-conditioning, now measurable — plus κ(AᵀA) = κ(A)² and the "10ᵏ loses k digits" rule. Energy: σ² sums to ‖M‖²F (50 = 45+5), so our M keeps 90% of itself in one direction. Then Eckart–Young, verified rather than cited: the best rank-1 approximation is [[1.5,1.5],[4.5,4.5]] and its error is 2.236068 = σ₂ exactly. Closes on structure-vs-noise (a solid 12×12 block is rank 1; a ramp is rank 2; gaussian noise needs 9 of 12 directions for 99% of its energy) and on the confusion worth killing: our M's eigenvalues are 3 and 5, its singular values 6.71 and 2.24 — different questions, different answers, coinciding only for symmetric PSD matrices.
newSVD ch. 4 — PCA, LoRA, denoising, MuonThe same four operations in different clothes. PCA = SVD of centred data, with a run showing σ²/(n−1) equals the covariance eigenvalues to the digit (5.3953, 0.0846) — and why practitioners avoid forming XᵀX (it squares κ). LoRA: r is the rank; B+A on a 4096×4096 weight is 0.391% of the parameters at r=8; the bet is that ΔW has a fast-decaying spectrum, and Eckart–Young bounds what you lose. Denoising, the most striking run in the course: a rank-2 signal plus noise, and reconstruction error bottoms out at k=2 (5.237) then gets worse at k=3, 5, 10 — throwing information away improves accuracy, and the elbow (15.8 ‖ 4.7) is the signal/noise boundary in plain sight. Then Muon decoded line by line against ch. 1–3, plus pseudo-inverse and whitening, eight review questions, and the whole course in four lines.

fixedMuon ch. 7 — why NOT embeddings, tested rather than assertedThe exclusion row used to hand-wave ("rows are lookup slots"). You asked whether it's about sparsity — so I tested that first, and it isn't: Newton–Schulz preserves zero rows exactly (every NS term ends in X, and A=XXᵀ has a zero row wherever X does; verified on a mostly-empty matrix). The real reason is the norm. Muon is steepest descent under RMS→RMS, whose derivation assumes dense activations — Bernstein's own "Deriving Muon" says that assumption is what "separates the Linear layer from, say, an Embedding layer that takes one-hot inputs." Corroboration that the axis is real: Muown (2026) names uncontrolled row-norm growth as the driver of Muon's spectral-norm drift even in hidden layers. Plus a new honesty table grading every claim: what's established (the practice, stated in Jordan's release), what's proved here (zero-row preservation, the ℓ₁→ℓ₂ identity), what's well-grounded-but-untested (the one-hot argument), what's weaker (my read of the output head), and what I could not find (a clean published ablation isolating the mechanism). 2026-08-01 Info theory ch. 4: KL rewritten in bits, and reverse KL tied to on-policy distillation

The chapter already had the "which distribution provides the weight" insight. What it lacked was the bits reading of both directions, the actual mechanics of forward-KL distillation, and any connection to on-policy distillation — which is the whole reason reverse KL matters in post-training.

Start here

Tab 3’s new opening card (both directions written as cross-entropy − entropy), then tab 4 top to bottom.

whatchange
newInfo theory ch. 4 tab 3 — both directions, in bitsForward = "bits to send the TEACHER’s words using the STUDENT’s code, minus the unavoidable ones"; reverse = "bits to send the STUDENT’s words using the TEACHER’s code, minus its own." Then the one rule that generates the whole asymmetry: whoever sits left of the ‖ is whose words you are sending — the right side only supplies the codebook. Foldable derives it from the 0·log(0/x) = 0 convention: forward forbids omission and permits invention; reverse forbids invention and permits omission.
newInfo theory ch. 4 tab 4 — forward distillation, mechanicallyCorrects the natural mental model: there is no student answer being compared. The teacher writes the text, and at every position the student is asked "what would you have predicted here?" — the per-position loss being the 1.43 bits the tab already computes. Plus the consequence: keep only the token the teacher wrote and the loss collapses to −log q(mat), so ordinary SFT is forward-KL distillation from an infinitely confident teacher; distillation’s soft target is what adds "floor was nearly as good."
newInfo theory ch. 4 tab 4 — the ∞ demo + on-policy distillationTeacher = [0.5, 0, 0.5] ("train" / nonsense blend / "taxi"). A hedging student scores 0.585 bits forward but ∞ reverse; a committing student scores ∞ forward but 1 bit reverse — each direction calls the other’s favourite infinitely bad. Then on-policy distillation: the two axes separated (whose text vs which direction), why sampling from the student automatically makes the KL reverse, and TM’s per-token advantage −(log π_student − log π_teacher) read as "extra bits the teacher spends on the token I chose." Closes with why "the teacher is correct" argues for reverse KL: at deployment the student writes the text, so what matters is whether everything it says is teacher-approved.
newInfo theory ch. 4 tab 3 — the notation papers useThe Σ p(x)·log(…) form this course writes, set beside the Ex∼π[…] form every paper writes, with the point that the subscript IS the weight moved inside the E — which is the entire reason one direction is reverse. Foldable glossary for π / θ / | / ∼ / E, anchored on the fact that ch. 1’s 1.75 bits was already an expectation. Ends with the asymmetry visible in a single token: invent (0.9 vs 0.1) contributes +2.85, omit (0.1 vs 0.9) contributes only −0.32, because the weight is the student’s.
newInfo theory ch. 4 tab 4 — who generates, who scoresOnly one model generates and it swaps: forward = teacher writes / student scores; reverse = student writes / teacher scores.
