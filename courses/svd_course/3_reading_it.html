SVD Chapter 3: Reading the Numbers

You can now compute a decomposition. This chapter is about what the numbers tell you — how many directions a matrix really has, how badly they disagree, how its content is distributed, and why truncating the list is the best compression that exists.

Rank: how many directions survive

Count the nonzero σ's. That's it.

A singular value of zero means one thing precisely: that direction is crushed to nothing. Feed in vᵢ and you get σᵢuᵢ = 0·uᵢ = 0 — the matrix has no opinion about it and no way to produce it. So:

rank(M) = the number of nonzero singular values

Geometrically, in chapter 1's picture: a zero σ means the ellipse is flat in that direction. A 2×2 with rank 1 doesn't map the circle to an ellipse at all — it maps it to a line segment.

See it: three matrices, three ranks

output — real run our M = [3 0; 4 5] singular values [6.7082, 2.2361] rank 2 # a real ellipse outer product e·xᵀ singular values [3.2241, 0.0000] rank 1 # a line segment

That second one is the object chapter 1 of the Muon chapter is built on: one training example's gradient. Every row of an outer product is the same vector rescaled, so every direction but one is crushed — and the SVD reports it as a single zero.

Why rank is a better question than it looks

"How many independent directions does this thing actually contain?" turns out to be the question behind a surprising number of practical situations:

situationthe rank fact
gradient of a batch of B examplesrank ≤ B — a batch of 32 gives a 4096×4096 weight at most 32 directions
a LoRA adapter with r = 16rank exactly 16 by construction: BA with inner dimension 16
solving Mx = ba zero σ means some b's are unreachable and some x's are invisible
a data matrix of 1,000 nearly-identical rowsnumerically rank ≈ 1 — you have one measurement, repeated

Careful though: exact zeros are a mathematician's luxury. In floating point you get 1e−17, not 0. Real code asks "how many σ are above a tolerance?" — which is why the next page's question, how small is small, matters more in practice than rank itself.

σ₁/σₙ: the number that has been haunting the optimizer course

Definition, and what it measures

κ(M) = σ₁ / σₙ # biggest stretch divided by smallest for our M: κ = 6.7082 / 2.2361 = 3.0

Read it off the ellipse: κ is how far from circular the output is. κ = 1 means a perfect circle — every direction treated identically. κ = 3 means our ellipse is three times longer than it is wide. κ = ∞ means it collapsed to a line (some σ is zero — that's rank deficiency).

This is exactly the optimizer course's disease, named

The optimizer course opened with a bowl L = w₁² + 10·w₂², curvatures 2 and 20, and called the ratio of 10 the "condition number" that forced SGD to zigzag. That was the same quantity as here — and now you can see why it is the deciding one:

κthe ellipsewhat one learning rate has to do
1a circleone lr serves every direction perfectly
1010× longer than widelr must be small enough for the steep direction → 10× too small for the flat one → zigzag
10⁶a sliverno usable single lr exists — this is the case adaptive optimizers were built for

The optimizer course said "one number cannot describe many directions." The SVD is what tells you how many directions there are and how badly they disagree. Same sentence, now measurable.

The connection is exact, not an analogy: gradient descent on a quadratic converges at a rate governed by the condition number of its curvature matrix, and the curvature matrix for a least-squares problem ‖Ax − b‖² is AᵀA — whose eigenvalues, from chapter 2, are exactly σ². So κ(AᵀA) = κ(A)²: squaring the matrix squares the difficulty.

The other thing κ predicts: how much precision you lose

κ is also the amplification factor for error. Nudge the input, and the output can move κ times more (relatively) than it should — because the nudge might land along the direction M barely stretches, while the answer lives along the one it stretches hard.

rule of thumb: solving with condition number 10ᵏ loses about k digits of accuracy κ = 10³ float32 (~7 digits) → ~4 good digits left κ = 10⁸ float32 → nothing left

This is why "ill-conditioned" is an insult in numerical computing, and why chapter 4's uses of the SVD are so often about detecting or repairing a bad κ.

σ² is a budget, and it always adds up to the whole matrix

The identity from chapter 2, taken seriously

output — real run ‖M‖²F = 3² + 0² + 4² + 5² = 50 σ₁² + σ₂² = 45 + 5 = 50

The sum of squared entries equals the sum of squared singular values. Since the left side is a natural measure of "how big is this matrix," the right side says: that size is divided among the directions, and σᵢ² is direction i's share. Turn it into percentages and the matrix tells you where it keeps its content:

direction 1: 45/50 = 90% direction 2: 5/50 = 10%

So 90% of what our M "is" lives along one direction. That is a strong statement about a matrix whose four entries looked unremarkable.

The payoff: throw away the small directions and see what breaks

If direction 2 holds only 10%, what happens if we simply delete it? Keep the first term of M = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + σ₂u₂v₂ᵀ and drop the second:

output — real run: A₁ = σ₁ u₁ v₁ᵀ A₁ = 6.7082 × [0.3162, 0.9487] ⊗ [0.7071, 0.7071] A₁ = [ 1.5 1.5 ] M = [ 3. 0. ] M − A₁ = [ 1.5 −1.5 ] [ 4.5 4.5 ] [ 4. 5. ] [ −0.5 0.5 ] ‖M − A₁‖F = 2.236068 σ₂ = 2.236068 identical

That is not a coincidence, and it is the most useful theorem attached to the SVD:

Eckart–Young: truncating the SVD after k terms gives the BEST possible rank-k approximation, and the error you pay is exactly σk+1.

"Best" is a strong word — among all rank-k matrices in existence, none is closer to M. So the SVD doesn't just describe a matrix; it hands you an optimal compression, sorted, with the error known in advance.

Structure has few directions; noise has many

Now the practical reading. Take three 12×12 matrices and count how many directions each needs to hold 99% of its energy:

output — real run: 12×12 matrices a solid SQUARE block σ = [6.000, 0.000, 0.000, …] rank 1 99% needs 1 of 12 an x+y RAMP σ = [143.9, 11.92, 0.000, …] rank 2 99% needs 1 of 12 pure NOISE (gaussian) σ = [6.281, 5.533, 4.733, 3.386, 3.279, 2.506, …] rank 12 99% needs 9 of 12

Read the spectra, not the matrices. A solid block is rank 1 — it is "this set of rows" times "this set of columns," one outer product. A ramp is rank 2. But noise has no structure to exploit, so its energy is spread almost evenly and there is nothing to throw away.

That is the whole of SVD-based compression, and also the whole of SVD-based denoising — the same act, judged differently: keep the top k directions, and you are either "compressing" (you wanted the structure) or "denoising" (you wanted to discard the rest). A fast decaying spectrum means the matrix has structure; a flat one means it doesn't.

Why is a solid rectangular block exactly rank 1?

Because you can write it as an outer product: let r be 1 for the rows the block covers and 0 elsewhere, and c the same for columns. Then the block is r·cᵀ — entry (i,j) is 1 exactly when row i and column j are inside. One outer product, so by page 0, rank 1. The single σ = 6.0 is then ‖r‖·‖c‖ = √6·√6, since the block is 6×6.

The confusion worth clearing up: SVD is not eigendecomposition

Our own matrix proves they're different

M = [3 0; 4 5] is triangular, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal: 3 and 5. Its singular values are 6.7082 and 2.2361. Not similar, not rescaled — unrelated.

eigendecompositionSVD
equationM v = λ v (same vector out)M v = σ u (a different vector out)
exists forsquare matrices, and not alwaysevery matrix, including non-square
directionsgenerally not perpendicularalways perpendicular, both sides
valuescan be negative, or complexalways real and ≥ 0
asks"which directions are unmoved?""which directions are stretched most?"

The questions are different, so the answers are. Eigenvectors ask what a matrix leaves pointing the same way — a question that only makes sense if input and output live in the same space, which is why non-square matrices have no eigenvectors at all. Singular vectors ask what it stretches, which always makes sense.

When they DO coincide — and why that's the case you usually meet

If M is symmetric and positive semi-definite, the two decompositions agree: σᵢ = λᵢ and uᵢ = vᵢ. That is the case for covariance matrices, Gram matrices, and every MᵀM — which is why the two ideas get blurred in practice.

And the bridge is chapter 2's derivation, now readable as a summary of the whole course:

MᵀM = (USVᵀ)ᵀ(USVᵀ) = V S Uᵀ U S Vᵀ = V S² Vᵀ # UᵀU = I ^ an eigendecomposition: eigenvectors V, eigenvalues σ² MMᵀ = (USVᵀ)(USVᵀ)ᵀ = U S Vᵀ V S Uᵀ = U S² Uᵀ # VᵀV = I

So the SVD of M is secretly two eigendecompositions — V from MᵀM, U from MMᵀ, and one shared set of σ². That is precisely the machinery chapter 2 used to compute everything by hand.

Carry forward

  • rank = count of nonzero σ. A zero σ means a crushed direction; an outer product has rank 1.
  • κ = σ₁/σₙ = how far from circular. It is the optimizer course's ill-conditioning, measured — and it predicts digits lost.
  • σᵢ² is a share of the matrix: they sum to ‖M‖²F. Ours splits 90% / 10%.
  • Eckart–Young: truncating after k terms is the best rank-k approximation, and the error is exactly σk+1 — verified: ‖M − A₁‖ = 2.236068 = σ₂.
  • Fast-decaying spectrum = structure (compressible). Flat spectrum = noise (nothing to throw away).
  • SVD ≠ eigendecomposition: for our M, eigenvalues 3 and 5, singular values 6.71 and 2.24. They agree only for symmetric PSD matrices.
