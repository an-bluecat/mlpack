Give every weight its own yardstick.

AdaGrad → RMSProp. Momentum remembers which way to go. Now we’ll build a memory for how big a gradient usually is.

The current seven-section visual lesson.

Same learning rate. A thousand times the movement.

Two weights receive gradients −24 and −0.024. With lr = 0.1, SGD turns that difference straight into a difference in step size.

These are illustrative gradient values, not a claim that one layer always has larger gradients than another. A shared learning rate can be awkward when useful changes along different coordinates require very different multipliers.

Δw = −lr × today’s gradientthis weight’s gradient scaleDo this separately for every weight. The positive divisor changes the size of the update, while the minus sign makes it oppose the gradient.

Measure a gradient relative to its own history, instead of treating its raw magnitude as the whole story.

Pause and predict

If both gradients are negative, which way do both weights move?

Reveal the reasoning

Both increase: subtracting a negative update adds to the weight. The pictures show positive movement. Normalization preserves that sign.

Refresher: is equal movement always the goal?

No. Gradient scale is not the same as curvature, distance to the minimum, or importance. Normalization reduces sensitivity to persistent differences in gradient scale; it does not guarantee that equal steps are optimal. An unusual gradient can still produce an unusual step.

And 0.3162 is deliberately larger than 0.1. We’ll explain that first-step surprise in the RMSProp section.

A snapshot loses what a history can tell you.

The obvious shortcut is to divide by today’s magnitude: g / |g|. That leaves only +1 or −1, so every nonzero gradient takes a full learning-rate step.

Keep a yardstick that outlasts today.

A historical scale lets a small gradient produce a small step relative to recent gradients. Squaring the gradients keeps opposite signs from canceling when we measure size.

Now make one day unusual.

The useful ratio is “how big is today compared with my history?” A historical denominator can preserve that comparison.

Refresher: why not divide by the squared size directly?

For a steady gradient of 24, the mean square is 576. Dividing 24 by 576 gives 1/24: it reverses the scale problem. Taking the square root first gives 24/24 = 1. Squares measure size without cancellation; the root restores the original units.

What does keeping magnitude buy us with noisy gradients?

Suppose +1.02 and −0.98 occur equally often. Their mean is +0.02, but their signs average to zero. Dividing both by a fixed historical scale of 1 preserves the +0.02 mean. That illustrates information lost by sign normalization. A denominator updated from the same noisy gradients can be correlated with them, so this is not a guarantee of an unbiased adaptive update.

AdaGrad’s memory only grows.

Give each weight one accumulator, G. Add its squared gradient every update. Divide by the square root of that total.

G ← G + g²
Δw = −lr × g / (√G + ε)
Start G at zero. ε is a small positive number to keep the division well behaved; we’ll return to it.

The root of a sum is not the RMS: √(Σg²) = √t × √(mean g²). AdaGrad normalizes the gradient scale and introduces shrinking steps when squared gradients keep accumulating.

Pause and predict

A weight has accumulated G = 400. Ten updates bring zero gradients. What is G now?

Reveal the reasoning

Still 400. AdaGrad never subtracts from its memory. The weight takes no steps while g = 0, and its divisor remains √400 = 20.

The same memory can help rare features.

If one weight has received 100 gradients of magnitude 2 and another only 4, their divisors are 20 and 4. On those respective active updates, their movements are 0.01 and 0.05. The less frequently updated weight retains a larger multiplier.

AdaGrad remembers every contribution. That can help rare features, but old large gradients can keep later steps small.

Refresher: does AdaGrad always decay as 1/√t?

That exact relation is for constant-magnitude gradients with a zero initial accumulator and negligible ε. In general G grows only when nonzero gradients arrive. Near a minimum, gradients may shrink fast enough that the accumulated sum approaches a finite value. AdaGrad can then settle because its numerator goes to zero while its divisor remains positive.

Check the update in PyTorch
import torch

w = torch.tensor([2.0], dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adagrad([w], lr=0.1, eps=1e-8,
                         initial_accumulator_value=0.0, lr_decay=0.0)
for _ in range(4):
    w.grad = torch.tensor([-2.0], dtype=torch.float64)
    opt.step()
    print(f'{w.item():.4f}')
# 2.1000, 2.1707, 2.2284, 2.2784

This supplies the same gradient on purpose to isolate the accumulator. In training, backward() supplies gradients from the current loss.

Let old gradients fade.

RMSProp replaces AdaGrad’s sum with an exponential moving average (EMA). Every update keeps a fraction β of the old memory and adds a fraction 1−β of today’s square.

v ← βv + (1−β)g²
Δw = −lr × g / (√v + ε)
Here β = 0.9 and v starts at zero. This is uncentered RMSProp without momentum or bias correction.

Each g² enters v with weight 0.1. After k more updates, its weight is 0.1 × 0.9ᵏ. “About ten steps of memory” is a rough time scale, not a ten-item sliding window.

The first-step surprise: 0.3162.

Starting the EMA at zero makes its early values small. Dividing by a small estimate makes early steps large. For constant g, vₜ = (1−βᵗ)g². Dividing vₜ by 1−βᵗ corrects this initialization effect under a stationary second moment—a piece we’ll reuse in Adam.

Pause and predict

Keep lr = 0.1, but change β from 0.9 to 0.99. Is the uncorrected first step bigger or smaller?

Reveal the reasoning

Bigger: 0.1/√0.01 = 1.0, with negligible ε. More memory also means admitting less of the first sample. Long memory and startup behavior are connected.

Refresher: “effective learning rate” or “step size”?

The per-weight multiplier is lr/(√v + ε). The actual movement magnitude is that multiplier times |g|. These plots show movement, not the multiplier alone. RMSProp does not make every movement exactly lr; spikes, changing gradients, initialization, and ε all affect the ratio.

See a single spike fade out of the denominator

Feed both methods 20 gradients of 1, one gradient of 10, then gradients of 1 again. AdaGrad retains the spike’s full contribution. RMSProp gradually returns toward its ordinary scale of 1.

Normalizing scale does not guarantee settling.

Return to L = w². RMSProp can get very close to zero, then resume oscillating as its denominator shrinks. Watch the full run, not just the first reassuring stretch.

The disappearing divisor explains the comeback.

Since g = 2w, each update is wnew = w × [1 − 2lr/(√v + ε)]. While gradients are tiny, v decays. If √v + ε falls below lr, the bracket has magnitude greater than 1: small deviations can grow and flip sign.

There is no mysterious force pushing a weight away from an exact zero. At exactly w = 0, the gradient and update are zero. The instability concerns tiny nonzero deviations.

Adaptive scaling answers “how large relative to my history?” A learning-rate schedule separately controls how far to move late in training.

Refresher: where does the ±lr/2 cycle come from?

Suppose the iterates alternate symmetrically between +a and −a. Then g² = 4a² every time, so a settled EMA has √v = 2a. Crossing from +a to −a requires 2a = lr × 2a/(2a + ε). For a nonzero cycle, a = (lr−ε)/2, approximately lr/2 when ε is tiny. This identifies a possible cycle on this particular bowl; it does not prove that every initialization or hyperparameter choice reaches it.

Does the story change with minibatch noise or Adam?

Noise can keep gradients nonzero near a minimum, so constant learning rates can leave persistent fluctuations for SGD and adaptive methods alike. Adam adds a smoothed numerator and changes the dynamics, but that alone is not a general guarantee of convergence. A schedule is still a separate design choice. The plots here are deterministic so we can see the denominator’s role without mixing in noise.

ε puts a floor under the divisor.

Without ε, an untouched weight with g = 0 and v = 0 asks for 0/0. Adding ε makes its update zero and limits how large the effective multiplier can become.

g = 0, v = 0: 00 + ε = 0Without ε: 0/0 is undefined (NaN in floating-point arithmetic).

For example, with ε = 10⁻⁸ and a steady |g| = 10⁻¹⁰, the movement is about 0.0099 × lr. Without ε, the same normalization would produce a full lr-sized movement.

ε prevents division by zero and limits amplification. It does not know whether a small gradient is signal or noise.

Pause and predict

A gradient is very small. Does that prove it is just rounding error?

Reveal the reasoning

No. Small gradients can be meaningful; their absolute size depends on how the problem is scaled. ε is a numerical and optimization parameter, not an automatic detector of useless gradients.

Refresher: is √v + ε the same as √(v + ε)?

No. At v = 0 and ε = 10⁻⁸, the first divisor is 10⁻⁸ and the second is 10⁻⁴. The same number has a different effect when placed inside the root. This chapter consistently uses sqrt(v) + eps, as in PyTorch RMSprop.

What changes in lower precision?

The dtype used for the optimizer state and division matters. For example, 10⁻⁸ rounds to zero in float16, so it cannot protect that division if the whole calculation is actually performed in float16. Mixed-precision training may keep optimizer state in float32. Check the arithmetic and implementation instead of inferring an ε choice from the model dtype alone.

Two memories. Two different jobs.

You now have the pieces for Adam: a memory for direction in the numerator and a memory for scale in the denominator.

Final check, without looking back

Why square? Why root? Why remember? Why forget?

Reveal the four answers

Square: measure size without sign cancellation. Root: return to gradient units. Remember: compare today with a longer-lived scale. Forget: let the scale follow recent gradients instead of accumulating forever.

Two controls remain: lr sets the overall movement scale; ε floors the divisor. Neither is replaced by adaptation.
