LaProp — the division Adam does too late

Chapter 6. Adam contains one silent design choice: momentum first, divide after. This chapter moves the division — and in doing so shows exactly where Adam can explode, and why its two β knobs were never independent.

Adam made an ordering choice nobody discusses

Look at Adam's assembly line again — where exactly is the division?

Chapter 4's carry-forward ended with a pocket note: m averages raw gradients, and the ÷√v̂ happens at the very end. Write the pipeline as stations:

# ADAM: average first, divide after m ← β₁·m + (1−β₁)·grad # station 1: average RAW gradients w ← w − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) # station 2: divide the AVERAGE by TODAY's yardstick

Now notice there was another place the division could have gone — normalize each gradient the moment it arrives, then average:

# LAPROP: divide first, average after m ← β₁·m + (1−β₁)·grad / (√v̂ + ε) # station 1: normalize TODAY's gradient by TODAY's yardstick, # then average the normalized pushes w ← w − lr · m̂ # station 2: the average IS the step — nothing left to divide

Same two memories (v is updated identically in both, β₂ EMA of g², bias-corrected), same cost, same three lines. Only the order of "average" and "divide" swapped. That swap is the whole of LaProp (Ziyin, Wang & Ueda 2020) — the name reads Learning-rate-adjusted Propagation, RMSProp's division welded onto each gradient before momentum ever sees it.

Say what each m means in words — they are not the same object

Adam's mLaProp's m
an EMA ofraw gradients — what the loss said, in gradient unitsnormalized steps — what I actually did, in lr units
unitsgradient units (could be 24, could be 0.024)dimensionless, each term already ≈ ±1
converted to a step bydividing by today's √v̂ — old pushes re-priced at today's exchange ratenothing — each push was priced the day it happened

The metaphor that survives scrutiny: Adam saves foreign currency and converts the whole stash at whatever today's rate is. LaProp converts each day's pay the day it arrives and saves the converted amount. If the exchange rate never moves, identical. If it moves…

First, the honest null result: constant scale ⇒ same optimizer

The algebra — one line

Suppose √v̂ settles at a constant c (steady gradients — chapter 3's steady state). An EMA is a weighted average, and dividing every term of an average by the same c is the same as dividing the average by c:

LaProp: m = EMA(g₁/c, g₂/c, …) = EMA(g₁, g₂, …)/c = Adam's m̂/√v̂

Division and averaging commute when the divisor is constant. So on any problem where each parameter's gradient scale holds still, the ordering is invisible.

And the run agrees — to six decimals

This is why the ordering went unexamined for years: on calm benchmarks the two are numerically indistinguishable. The difference can only live where the yardstick √v̂ moves — which is exactly late training, distribution shifts, RL, and every β₂ that forgets fast. Next page: make the yardstick move and watch Adam misbehave.

Make the scale move: gradients that shrink as you converge

The scenario — nothing adversarial about it

Gradients shrinking steadily is what convergence looks like. Take gt = 0.8ᵗ — each step the gradient is 80% of the last. Now walk what each memory does, remembering chapter 4's horizon rule (memory length ≈ 1/(1−β)):

numerator m: β₁ = 0.9 → remembers ~10 steps → still full of OLD, BIG gradients denominator v: β₂ = 0.5 → remembers ~2 steps → has already FORGOTTEN them Adam step = (old big pushes) / (today's small yardstick) = grows

The numerator decays like the gradient 10 steps ago; the denominator decays like today's. Their ratio grows by a factor ≈ β₁/√β₂ every step. For β₁=0.9, β₂=0.5 that's 0.9/√0.5 = 1.273 — a 27% larger step every step, forever.

Read t=25 twice. The gradient has died to 0.0038 — the landscape is essentially flat — and Adam is taking steps of 10× lr and accelerating: the step is ~2,700× the gradient that provoked it. LaProp glides down from 1.0 toward a floor. Nothing about the loss demanded this; it is purely the two memories decaying at mismatched speeds.

So Adam's two knobs were never independent

The growth factor β₁/√β₂ must stay below 1, so Adam is only stable when

β₁ ≤ √β₂ # Adam's hidden coupling rule

Check the presets against it:

output — real run: same experiment, default β₂ β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999: β₁/√β₂ = 0.900 < 1 → Adam decays fine: 1.00 → 0.91 → 0.71 → 0.22 β₁=0.9, β₂=0.5 : β₁/√β₂ = 1.273 > 1 → the table above

The defaults sit safely inside the rule — that's why you've never seen this. But the rule means you cannot shorten the scale memory without endangering the trend memory: they're one knob wearing two labels. LLM pretraining already walks toward the edge (β₂=0.95 → √β₂ = 0.9747, margin thinning), and any research that wants a fast-forgetting yardstick — noisy rewards, shifting data — is walled off entirely. That is the coupling LaProp's title says it removes.

Where β₁ ≤ √β₂ comes from — the two-line bound, with numbers

Step 1: one gradient can only be so big relative to its own v. Since v ≥ (1−β₂)·g² (today's contribution alone), |g|/√v ≤ 1/√(1−β₂) — for β₂=0.5 that's 1.414, for 0.999 it's 31.62. Step 2: Adam's ratio m/√v is a sum of terms (β₁ⁱ·gt−i)/√vt, and in the worst case v decayed for i steps while β₁ⁱ decayed slower: each term ≤ (β₁/√β₂)ⁱ/√(1−β₂). Geometric series: converges iff β₁/√β₂ < 1, to the bound 1/(√(1−β₂)·(1−β₁/√β₂)) — for the defaults, a finite but loose 317× lr. Above 1, no bound exists, and the 0.8ᵗ run is the constructive proof.

LaProp's guarantee: bounded for any β₁, β₂ — derived, then observed

The derivation is two sentences now

Each ingredient entering LaProp's momentum is already normalized by its own contemporary yardstick:

each term: |g/√v̂| ≤ 1/√(1−β₂) # chapter-3 fact: v holds at least (1−β₂)·g² of TODAY m = EMA(bounded things) ≤ the same bound # an average can't exceed its ingredients step ≤ lr/√(1−β₂) for ANY β₁, β₂ # β₂=0.5 → 1.414·lr. No coupling rule needed.

Adam's failure needed old gradients re-priced by a new yardstick. LaProp never re-prices: by the time a gradient enters the average it is already a finished, bounded step. The 0.8ᵗ table confirms it: LaProp's column never exceeds 1.000 and settles at 0.68, comfortably under the 1.414 ceiling — with the exact β pair that sent Adam to 10× lr and climbing.

What the freed knobs buy: LaProp interpolates between two families

With the coupling gone, the corners of (β₁, β₂) space become legal — and they're optimizers you already know:

β₂ → 0: v = g² → g/√v̂ = sign(g) → m = EMA of signs = sign descent + momentum # chapter 3's "failed first try" — now SAFE, because momentum averages the signs # (this corner, polished, is the Lion family) β₁ = 0: m = today's g/√v̂ → bias-corrected RMSProp, exactly (= Adam with β₁=0)

Slide β₂ between 0 and 1 and you morph continuously from signed-gradient methods to adaptive methods. Adam cannot make this trip: its version of the β₂→0 corner divides decade-old raw gradients by today's |g| — unbounded, the page-2 disease in its purest form.

Bias correction in LaProp — same patches, one practical wrinkle

Both memories get the chapter-4 honesty patches: v̂ = v/(1−β₂ᵗ) before the division, and the momentum average is un-shrunk by 1/(1−β₁ᵗ). One wrinkle real implementations handle (timm's laprop.py): if lr changes over training (warmup, cosine), each buffered term was implicitly priced at its lr — so the buffer stores lr·(normalized step) and the correction tracks an EMA of past lrs rather than the closed form 1−β₁ᵗ. At constant lr the two are identical; the chapter's traces use constant lr.

Honest practice card

  • LaProp did not displace AdamW. At default βs the two run near-identically (page 1), so inertia wins. Its adoption is research-side: noisy objectives, aggressive β₂, optimizer analysis.
  • Its lasting value is diagnostic: it isolates the one design decision inside Adam that can blow up — and gives a concise explanation of the failure mode. "Adam's momentum stores raw gradients and re-prices them with today's √v̂; if v forgets faster than m, old pushes get amplified without bound — the fix is normalizing before averaging, which is LaProp."
  • When you see a loss spike late in training with a lowered β₂, you now have a specific suspect — not "Adam is unstable" but which division, applied when.

Carry forward

  • Adam = average then divide; LaProp = divide then average. Identical while √v̂ is constant; different exactly when the scale moves.
  • Adam's hidden rule: β₁ ≤ √β₂, or steps grow by β₁/√β₂ per step (0.8ᵗ run: 10×lr by t=25, on a dying gradient).
  • LaProp's guarantee: every step ≤ lr/√(1−β₂), any βs — bounded because each term is normalized before it's remembered.
  • β₂→0 turns LaProp into momentum-of-signs — chapter 3's failed first try, made safe.
  • One blind spot still standing, shared by every row of the table: each weight is treated as a lone scalar. A weight matrix has structure none of these can see — that's chapter 7.
