The second disease, at its real scale

Two parameters, gradients 1000× apart

Forget curvature for a moment — here's the version of the problem you meet in every real network. One parameter sits in a hot path (say, a LayerNorm gain touched by every token): gradient −24. Another sits in a cold path (an embedding row for a rare word): gradient −0.024. Same model, same loss, same lr=0.1. What SGD does:

output — real run g= -24.000: SGD lr=0.1 step = 2.4000 g= -0.024: SGD lr=0.1 step = 0.0024

The hot parameter takes a wild 2.4-unit leap (remember chapter 1: that's deep in overshoot territory). The cold one moves by 0.0024 — it would need a thousand steps to move what the hot one moved in one. One lr cannot serve both. Lower it and the cold parameter freezes entirely; raise it and the hot one explodes. This is chapter 1's bowl again, but now with a spread of scales no hand-tuning can bridge.

State the wish precisely

What we want: every parameter should move about lr per step — regardless of how big its gradient happens to be. The gradient should choose the direction; the learning rate should choose the distance; the gradient's magnitude should stop tyrannizing the step size.

The move that achieves it: divide each parameter's step by (something like) the typical size of its own gradient. Big-gradient parameters get divided by a big number, small-gradient parameters by a small one — both land near lr. The chapter is about what "typical size" should mean.

The obvious first try — divide by TODAY's gradient. Watch it fail three ways

Before any history-keeping: the simplest reading of "divide by its own size" is divide by today's size. That has a closed form:

w ← w − lr · grad / |grad| = w − lr · sign(grad)

Every parameter moves exactly lr, every step, no matter what. The wish, granted trivially — and this is a real method (sign descent; Lion, 2023, is a polished version). But watch what dividing by today destroys:

Failure 1 — it cannot slow down at the bottom. Near the minimum the gradient shrinks toward 0, but sign(g) is still ±1, so the step is still a full lr. On the 1-D bowl L=w², start w=0.83, lr=0.1:

output — real run sign: 0.730, 0.630, 0.530, 0.430 ... then forever: -0.070, 0.030, -0.070, 0.030 SGD : 0.664, 0.531, 0.425, 0.340 ... step 25: 0.003 (still shrinking)

Sign descent marches down beautifully, then bounces across the minimum forever — it erased the one signal ("gradient is getting small") that says you're close, ease off.

Failure 2 — it erases "is today unusual?". Divide by today's size and every day looks the same. Divide by the typical size (root of the mean square so far) and a spike stands out while an ordinary day steps small:

output — real run: gradients 1, 1, 1, 10, 1 t g g/|g| typical = √(mean g² so far) g/typical 1 1 +1 1.000 1.000 4 10 +1 5.074 1.971 ← spike SEEN: ~2× step 5 1 +1 4.561 0.219 ← caution after the shock

The ratio today / typical is information — sign descent throws it away; dividing by history keeps it.

Failure 3 — noise. Minibatch gradients are noisy. Take a true gradient of +0.02 buried in ±1.0 batch noise: g is +1.02 or −0.98, equally likely — so E[sign(g)] = 0, exactly. Sign descent takes full-size steps in a direction that averages to nothing. Dividing by the typical size (≈1.0, the noise scale) gives steps of ≈ lr·g/1.0 — small, and their average preserves the +0.02 signal:

output — real run: 2,000 steps × 20 seeds, lr=0.1 (ideal progress = 4.00) sign descent net move: −0.5 (spread ±4.0 — pure random walk) divide-by-RMS net move: +3.1 (spread ±4.0 — the signal survives)

So "typical size" cannot mean today. It must mean a memory of this parameter's own past — big enough to cancel scale, dumb enough to leave today's news visible. The rest of the chapter is two proposals for that memory.

AdaGrad: remember every gradient ever — and pay for it

The update (2011)

G ← G + grad² # per-parameter running SUM of squared gradients w ← w − lr · grad / (√G + ε) # divide by the root of the history

√G is the "typical gradient size so far" (root of the accumulated square). A parameter that has seen gradients of ~24 has √G ≈ 24·√t; one that's seen ~0.024 has √G ≈ 0.024·√t — each gets divided by its own scale, and the wish from page 0 comes true. AdaGrad was built for exactly the rare-word-embedding case, and it works.

Check it against page 0's failed first try: on the very first step G = g², so √G = |g| and the step is lr·g/|g| — AdaGrad's step 1 IS sign descent. The two methods only part ways at step 2, when the denominator becomes history instead of today. Everything page 0 wanted from "memory" arrives exactly there: √(g₁²+g₂²) can register that today is calmer than yesterday; |g₂| alone cannot.

The fatal flaw: the sum only grows

G adds a positive number every step and never forgets. So √G grows like √t forever, and the effective step shrinks like 1/√t — even if you're nowhere near done. Watch it with a constant gradient (nothing is converging; the landscape isn't changing; the optimizer just… ages):

output — real run: constant g = −24, lr = 0.1 step 1: effective step = 0.1000 step 2: effective step = 0.0707 step 10: effective step = 0.0316 step 100: effective step = 0.0100

By step 100 the optimizer moves at a tenth of its starting speed, purely because time passed. For convex problems that's even provably the right schedule; for deep learning — where you may need full-speed steps at epoch 50 when the loss landscape shifts — it's a slow death. The idea (divide by gradient scale) is right; the memory (infinite) is wrong.

But don't file this as a pure defect — it is a trade, and it buys something. An optimizer whose step decays on a schedule can settle. Run AdaGrad on chapter 1's bowl and it does what sign descent could not:

output — real run: L = w², w₀ = 0.83, lr = 0.1 AdaGrad t= 200: |w| = 7.076e-05 # sign descent, same t: |w| ≈ 0.05, forever AdaGrad t= 1,000: |w| = 1.671e-20 AdaGrad t=20,000: |w| = 5.929e-323 # it lands, and stays landed

The very 1/√t that starves it at epoch 50 is what lets it come to rest at the bottom. Hold that thought for exactly one page: RMSProp removes the decay — and gives back the landing.

RMSProp: same idea, sliding-window memory

One-symbol fix: replace the sum with a decaying average

v ← β·v + (1−β)·grad² # EMA of squared gradients (β = 0.9: last ~10 steps) w ← w − lr · grad / (√v + ε) # divide by the RECENT typical size

This is chapter 2's memory trick applied to the denominator: v is an exponential moving average (the (1−β) makes it a true average — it settles at the typical g², not 10× it). Old gradients fade instead of accumulating, so the effective lr tracks the recent gradient scale and never dies of old age. The name says exactly what it divides by: Root Mean Square, Propagated over time.

The wish, granted — real numbers

output — real run: first step, lr = 0.1, β = 0.9 g= -24.000: SGD step = 2.4000 | RMSProp step = 0.3162 g= -0.024: SGD step = 0.0024 | RMSProp step = 0.3162

Look at the right column: identical steps for gradients 1000× apart. Trace the big one by hand: v = 0.9·0 + 0.1·(−24)² = 57.6; √v = 7.589; step = 0.1·24/7.589 = 0.3162. The tiny one: v = 0.1·(0.024)² → √v = 0.00759 → same 0.3162. Each parameter is divided by its own recent scale, so the scale cancels — direction survives, magnitude is normalized. Page 0's wish, delivered.

But squint at that number: 0.3162 ≠ lr = 0.1

The wish said "move about lr." We got 3.16× lr. Why: on the very first step, v = 0.1·g² — the EMA has only collected 10% of one sample, so it underestimates the typical g² by 10×, and √v underestimates by √10 ≈ 3.16 — inflating the step by exactly that. A freshly-initialized EMA lies small; dividing by a lie-small number means stepping too big, right at the start of training when weights are random and caution matters most.

Remember 0.3162. Fixing precisely this number — by explicitly correcting the young EMA's underestimate — is one of the two things Adam adds in the next chapter.

The whole chapter is one picture — feed both optimizers the same constant gradient and just watch the effective step over time:

Refresher: why divide by the SQUARE ROOT of v, not v itself?

Units. v averages g², so its natural scale is (gradient)² — for g ≈ 24, v ≈ 576. Divide g by v and you'd get 24/576 = 1/24: the step would shrink for big gradients and blow up for tiny ones far too aggressively (1000× spread becomes 1000× the other way). Take the root first and √v ≈ |g|, so g/√v ≈ ±1 — a clean, dimensionless "which way, in units of my own typical size." Then lr alone sets the distance. Rule of thumb: anything that averages squares must be un-squared before it meets the gradient.

The bill for removing the decay: RMSProp inherits page 0's Failure 1

Page 0 killed sign descent partly because it cannot slow down at the bottom. Fair question, and the chapter owes you the answer: does dividing by a decaying average fix that? Run RMSProp on the very same bowl that exposed sign descent — L = w², w₀ = 0.83, lr = 0.1:

output — real run t= 1 |w| = 5.138e-01 # the 0.3162 overshoot t= 16 |w| = 5.877e-05 # it IS slowing down — beautifully t= 40 |w| = 3.093e-19 # converged, for all practical purposes t= 80 |w| = 1.618e-03 # ... climbing back OUT? t=120 |w| = 6.203e-02 t=200 |w| = 5.014e-02 # settles here and never leaves

So the answer is no — and the way it fails is worth more than the fact that it fails. RMSProp does slow down (the ratio g/√v falls 3.16 → 0.058 → ~0), converges to 10⁻¹⁹ — and is then kicked back out, landing in a permanent oscillation. Where does it land? Sweep lr:

output — real run: final |w| after 400 steps lr = 0.20 → 0.1000 lr = 0.05 → 0.0250 lr = 0.01 → 0.0050 lr = 0.10 → 0.0500 lr = 0.02 → 0.0100 # every row: |w| = lr/2, to 3 decimals

|w| = lr/2 — the exact orbit sign descent settles into (its cycle spans −0.070 … 0.030 at lr = 0.1: RMS 0.054). Same destination, reached in 100 steps instead of 8.

Why the kick-out? Trace the two quantities separately. Once w is tiny the parameter stops moving, so g holds still — but v does not: with nothing to feed it, the EMA keeps shrinking by β = 0.9 each step, so √v decays ×0.949 per step forever. A fixed numerator over a denominator sliding toward zero:

output — real run: the ratio climbing back t= 40 g = 5.16e-19 √v = 9.10e-02 ratio = 0.0000 # asleep t= 80 g = 3.38e-03 √v = 1.02e-01 ratio = 0.0331 # the yardstick has shrunk; steps resume t=100 g = 4.00e-02 √v = 9.24e-02 ratio = 0.4327 # feedback: bigger step → bigger g → bigger step t=120 g = 1.26e-01 √v = 1.01e-01 ratio = 1.2477 # ratio ≈ ±1 — sign descent, in all but name

Not "it never slowed down" but "it slowed down, then forgot why." The equilibrium is where the two decays balance, and the ratio sits at ≈ ±1 — precisely the state page 0 called a failure. (Not an ε artifact: rerun with ε from 1e−16 to 1e−4 and the answer is 5.01e−02 every time.)

This is the same trade as the previous page, read from the other side. AdaGrad's 1/√t is a permanent brake: it lands, but it dies of old age. RMSProp releases the brake: it stays alive forever, and can no longer land.

output — real run: |w| at t = 200, same bowl, lr = 0.1 SGD 3.4e-20 lands # step ∝ g, and g → 0 AdaGrad 7.1e-05 lands # the 1/√t "flaw" IS the brake Adam 1.8e-05 lands # see the foldable — for a reason you'll meet again in ch. 6 sign 5.4e-02 orbits at lr/2 RMSProp 5.0e-02 orbits at lr/2

Which brings up the thing nobody tells you when they hand you Adam: an adaptive optimizer does not anneal itself, so you must anneal it. That is what a learning-rate schedule is for — not a tuning nicety but the brake AdaGrad had built in and RMSProp gave away. In real (noisy) training the effect is unmissable, because minibatch noise never shrinks: near a minimum √v is pinned at the noise scale, g/√v ≈ ±1, and the parameters diffuse in a ball rather than converging.

output — real run: noisy bowl (g = 2w + 𝒩(0, 0.1)), RMS|w| over the last 500 of 3,000 steps, 8 seeds constant lr lr decayed linearly to 0 SGD 0.0170 0.0047 Adam 0.0447 0.0141 RMSProp 0.0621 0.0147

Every optimizer's noise ball shrinks 3–4× from the schedule alone — same weights, same gradients, same everything else. Adaptive methods buy you scale-blindness, and the price is that the last mile is yours to schedule.

Then why did Adam land? (a preview of the coupling rule)

Because Adam's numerator has a memory too, and it forgets faster than the yardstick does. When gradients shrink geometrically, m decays like β₁ᵗ = 0.9ᵗ while √v decays like √β₂ᵗ = 0.9995ᵗ — so the ratio m/√v shrinks and Adam brakes itself to 3.5e−92 by t = 4,000. RMSProp has no numerator memory: its "m" is today's raw g, which freezes when w freezes, while √v keeps sliding. The governing quantity is β₁ vs √β₂ — the same inequality, in the same direction, that chapter 6 shows can make Adam explode when it points the other way. One rule, two opposite consequences. And note this is a deterministic rescue: on the noisy bowl above, Adam's ball is 0.0447 — it still needs the schedule.

ε — not decoration. It's the line between signal and dust

Every update in this chapter ends in / (√v + ε). Two separate jobs hide in that ε, and both are checkable.

Job 1 — stop one specific NaN. A parameter can receive an exactly zero gradient for many steps — a dead ReLU's weights, an embedding row no batch has touched, a padded position. Then v = 0, and without ε:

output — real run eps=0, g=0, v=0: step = lr · 0/√0 = nan then: w −= nan → w = nan. nan·0.9 = nan, nan+5 = nan — nothing recovers it.

One 0/0 and that weight is poisoned for the rest of training — and everything it touches downstream goes NaN with it. ε makes the same case read 0/(0+1e-8) = 0: a parameter with no gradient takes no step. Sane.

Job 2 — refuse to amplify numerical dust. The whole point of dividing by √v is to equalize: any gradient, however small, gets scaled up to a ~lr step. But "however small" is a bug, not a feature — a gradient of 10⁻¹⁰ is not information, it's float rounding noise, and equalizing would hand it a full-size step. Watch what the ε in the denominator does across scales (steady state, where √v = |g|):

output — real run: step multiplier |g|/(|g|+ε), ε = 1e-8 g = 24 → step = lr · 1.000000 ┐ g = 0.024 → step = lr · 1.000000 ├ signal: fully equalized g = 1e-6 → step = lr · 0.990099 ┘ g = 1e-8 → step = lr · 0.500000 ← the crossover: g ≈ ε g = 1e-10 → step = lr · 0.009901 ┐ dust: quietly ignored g = 0 → step = lr · 0.000000 ┘

Read the column: above ε, every gradient is normalized to lr; below ε, the division stops mattering and the update degrades to ≈ lr·g/ε — plain SGD with a fixed multiplier, which for dust-sized g is a dust-sized step. ε is the optimizer's definition of "too small to be real" — the threshold where it stops equalizing and starts ignoring.

That's also why ε is a real tunable, not a constant of nature: in float16 mixed precision, gradient noise sits far above 10⁻⁸, so the default threshold misclassifies noise as signal — raising ε to 1e-6 (or more) moves the line to where the dust actually is. If a loss spike ever traces back to one strange parameter group, checking ε is a legitimate move, not superstition.

Two memories now exist — put them on one map

Numerator memory vs denominator memory

Momentum (ch. 2)RMSProp (this chapter)
remembersthe gradients themselves (with sign)the gradients' squares (size only)
lives in the update asthe numerator — what direction, how hardthe denominator — the per-parameter yardstick
fixeszigzag & noise over time; starved slow directionsscale spread across parameters
charactera ball: bold, can overshootan equalizer: calm, scale-blind

They are not rivals — they answer different questions ("which way is the trend?" vs "how big is a normal step for this particular weight?") and they compose naturally: keep an EMA of gradients for the numerator, an EMA of squared gradients for the denominator, divide. That composition — plus one honesty patch for young EMAs (your 0.3162) — has a name, and it's the default optimizer of the deep-learning era.

Carry forward

  • The wish: every parameter moves ~lr; its gradient picks direction, not distance.
  • AdaGrad grants it by dividing by √(sum of g²) — but the infinite memory makes lr decay to zero on a schedule set by time, not progress.
  • RMSProp swaps in an EMA: divide by the recent RMS gradient. Equalizes 1000× scale spreads (real: both steps 0.3162).
  • Why history, not today: dividing by |today's g| is sign descent — can't slow down at the minimum, can't see spikes, extracts E[sign(g)] which noise can pin to zero. The memory is the cure, not a convenience.
  • ε = the dust threshold: above it, gradients are equalized to ~lr; below it, they're ignored. Also the guard against a permanent 0/0 → NaN.
  • The honest cost: AdaGrad's 1/√t is a brake (it lands: 1.7e−20 by t=1,000) and RMSProp gave it away — so RMSProp inherits page 0's Failure 1 and orbits at exactly |w| = lr/2, sign descent's own limit cycle. Not "never slowed down" but "slowed down, then the EMA forgot why."
  • Therefore: an adaptive optimizer does not anneal itself — the lr schedule is that brake, restored under your control. Noisy bowl, decay alone: 0.062 → 0.015.
  • The loose thread: a young EMA underestimates → first steps are ~3.16× too big. Carry the number 0.3162 into the next chapter.
