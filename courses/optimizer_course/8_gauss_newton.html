Gauss-Newton — the learning rate was a curvature all along

Chapter 8. Seven chapters of guessing, smoothing and rescaling a learning rate. This chapter computes the number the learning rate was always standing in for — and shows the one cheap way to get it that survives in every second-order method covered here.

The number chapter 1 already computed, read the other way round

Three numbers you own

Same model as chapters 1–6: prediction w·x, one data point x = 3, target t = 10, loss L = (w·x − t)². At w = 2:

pred = 2·3 = 6 residual = pred − t = 6 − 10 = −4 L = (−4)² = 16 gradient g = 2·(−4)·3 = −24 SGD, lr = .01: w ← 2 − 0.01·(−24) = 2.24 L = (6.72 − 10)² = 10.7584

Chapter 1 also found the ceiling: SGD diverges once lr · 18 > 2, i.e. lr > 0.111, and called 18 the curvature. That number is about to stop being a ceiling and become the step itself.

Step 1 — where is the bottom of this loss?

L(w) = (3w − 10)² is a parabola. Its bottom is where the thing being squared is zero: 3w = 10, so w = 10/3 = 3.333. From w = 2 that is a jump of 10/3 − 2 = 4/3 = 1.333. SGD at lr = .01 moved 0.24 of it.

Of course we can't in general "look at the loss and see its bottom." So: can 4/3 be produced from the things a training step actually has — the gradient and one more number?

Step 2 — the gradient is a line; find where the line is zero

Write the gradient as a function of w, not just at w = 2:

g(w) = 2·(3w − 10)·3 = 18w − 60 # a straight line with slope 18 check at w = 2: 18·2 − 60 = 36 − 60 = −24 ✓ g(w) = 0 ⇒ 18w = 60 ⇒ w = 10/3 ✓

Before H: the loss and its gradient are two different graphs

Keep the same horizontal axis in both pictures. The left picture asks how much loss a weight has. The right asks how steep that left-hand curve is at each weight.

The slope of the loss is g. The slope of g is H. On this quadratic, the gradient really is a straight line. On a general loss, Newton uses only the tangent to the gradient at the current point.

The slope of the gradient, 18, is the curvature from chapter 1 — that is what "curvature" means: how fast the gradient changes as you move. In general we know only two numbers at the current point: the gradient g and its slope, call it H. Pretend the gradient is a straight line (exact here, approximate elsewhere) and ask where that line hits zero:

Refresher: why is HΔ added, and why does Δ become −g/H?

A line predicts starting height + slope × horizontal movement. Here the starting height is g. Setting g + HΔ = 0 gives HΔ = −g. Divide by H, if H is nonzero. The target zero is the predicted gradient; neither Δ nor the loss is being set to zero.

g(w + Δ) ≈ g + H·Δ = 0 ⇒ Δ = −g / H = −(−24)/18 = 4/3 ⇒ w = 3.333, L = 0

That is Newton's method: the tangent of the gradient, set to zero. Now read the same step as SGD: Δ = −(1/18)·g. It is an SGD step with lr = 1/18 = 0.0556. The learning rate is 1/curvature. Chapter 1's ceiling, 2/curvature, was exactly twice the step that lands at the bottom.

Step 3 — two dimensions: the bowl in one step

The running bowl L = w₁² + 10·w₂², gradients (2w₁, 20w₂). The gradient's slope is now one number per direction: 2 along w₁, 20 along w₂. From the usual start (10, 1):

g = (2·10, 20·1) = (20, 20) Δ₁ = −20 / 2 = −10 Δ₂ = −20 / 20 = −1 ⇒ (10, 1) + (−10, −1) = (0, 0) as learning rates: lr₁ = 1/2 = 0.5 lr₂ = 1/20 = 0.05

Chapter 3 wished for "a different learning rate per parameter" and estimated one from gradient history. Newton doesn't estimate it — it reads it off the curvature, and gets it exactly.

Each column asks what happens when one weight moves. Each row names the gradient component being changed. For this axis-aligned bowl, H = [[2, 0], [0, 20]]. The zeros mean neither weight changes the other gradient component. The tilted example later will replace those zeros with coupling.

On the bowl the slopes are one per direction and independent. In general "how fast the gradient changes" is a whole table: H[i,j] = how much the gradient of parameter i changes when parameter j moves. That P×P table is the Hessian. Off-diagonal entries mean the parameters are coupled; page 3 shows what that looks like.

The catch — and what the chapter is about

  • Size. The Hessian has P² entries. For a 7B model that is 7×10&sup9; squared = 4.9×10¹&sup9; numbers — 196 exabytes in fp32. Nobody forms it.
  • Second derivatives. Backprop gives the gradient. The slope of the gradient is another pass, of a kind autograd does not do for free.
  • Sign. A curvature can be negative on a non-convex loss. Newton then solves "gradient = 0" faithfully — and lands on a hilltop. Page 2 shows it happen.

Gauss-Newton is a curvature you get from the first derivatives you already have, and that can never be negative. The price: it is exact only for a certain shape of loss, and approximate otherwise. Pages:

  • Page 1: the idea — take the tangent of the residual, not of the gradient.
  • Page 2: a model whose residual bends — Gauss-Newton converges in 4 steps, Newton walks uphill.
  • Page 3: two coupled parameters — the tilt that Adam's per-parameter numbers cannot fix.

  • Pages 5–6: neural networks — GGN, Fisher, and where Adam, Sophia, K-FAC, Shampoo and Muon sit on the same map.

Gauss-Newton: linearize the thing you square, not the gradient

First: where does 3w − 10 come from?

Start with a tiny model: prediction = weight × input, with no bias term. Our one training example has input x = 3 and target t = 10 (the answer we want the model to give). These two numbers are given data. w is the weight we adjust.

Build the expression one operation at a time

prediction = w × x = w × 3 = 3wsigned error = prediction − target = 3w − 10

3w means 3 multiplied by w. We subtract 10 to compare the prediction with the known target.

Try w = 2: the model predicts 2 × 3 = 6. The target is 10, so the signed error is 6 − 10 = −4. The minus sign says the prediction is 4 below the target.

Now give this signed prediction error a short name: the residual, written r(w) = 3w − 10. Read r(w) as “the error at weight w.” It is the same subtraction we just did.

Try weight wPrediction 3wResidual: prediction − 10
26−4: below the target
10/3100: matches the target
412+2: above the target

The loss squares this error: at w = 2, L = (−4)² = 16. So the residual is −4, while the loss is 16. The residual graph below plots these signed gaps against w; subtracting the fixed target shifts the blue prediction line down by 10.

J is the slope of the residual. H is the slope of the gradient. The names change because we are drawing a tangent to a different function.

Step 1 — the residual has a tangent too

The loss is a square of something. Give that something a name: the residual r(w) = pred − t = 3w − 10. At w = 2 it is −4. Its slope — how fast the residual changes per unit of w — is 3. Call the slope J (for Jacobian: with many residuals and many parameters it becomes a table, page 3; here it is one number).

tangent of r at w = 2: r(2 + Δ) ≈ r + J·Δ = −4 + 3Δ set it to zero: −4 + 3Δ = 0 ⇒ Δ = −r / J = 4/3 = 1.333

Same 4/3 as Newton on page 0 — from a different tangent. Newton drew the tangent of the gradient; this drew the tangent of the residual. This is the whole of Gauss-Newton, and the one story the rest of the chapter keeps: draw the residual's tangent, jump to where it is zero.

Step 2 — why the two tangents agree here, and the term that separates them elsewhere

Compute Newton's curvature H from the residual, one link at a time.

(1) L = r² (2) gradient, chain rule: g = 2·r·J # 2·(−4)·3 = −24 ✓ (3) curvature = slope of g. g is a product of r and J, so product rule: H = 2·( J·J + r·J′ ) # J′ = slope of J = how the residual's slope itself changes = r″ (4) H = 2J² + 2·r·r″ └ square term ┘ └ bend term ┘ (5) here r = 3w − 10 is a straight line: r″ = 0. H = 2·9 + 0 = 18 ✓

Why did differentiating once more produce two terms?

We already have g = 2rJ. Moving w can change r, and can also change J. The product rule counts both effects:

What changes?Its rate of changeContribution to the slope of g
The residual rdr/dw = J2 × J × J = 2J²
The residual’s slope JdJ/dw = r″2 × r × r″

For the line r = 3w − 10, J stays at 3, so the second row is zero. In the next example r = 3w² − 10, J = 6w changes as you move; that second row matters.

So the curvature has two parts. The square term 2J² needs only the residual's slope. The bend term 2·r·r″ needs the slope's slope, and it was zero on this model, which is why both tangents gave 4/3.

Gauss-Newton = Newton with the bend term dropped, always: HGN = 2J² step: Δ = −g / HGN = −(2·r·J) / (2·J²) = −r / J # the 2 cancels; the J cancels once

Read that last line: dropping the bend term from Newton gives back, exactly, "residual's tangent = 0." Those are not two methods; they are one method seen from two sides. Papers say "Newton with the Hessian approximated by JᵀJ"; the picture is "linearize the residual."

Step 3 — why dropping the bend is allowed (the entire sales pitch)

  • It uses first derivatives. In this scalar example, J is just the residual’s slope. For many outputs, the full Jacobian is not a free by-product of one backward pass; its rows or products require work. The omitted bend term additionally needs second derivatives.
  • It can be small near a good fit. If residuals approach zero while their second derivatives remain bounded, the bend term vanishes. At a best fit with nonzero residuals, it need not vanish; Gauss-Newton and Newton can still differ.
  • It can never be negative. 2J² is a square. Newton's H = 2J² + 2·r·r″ can go negative when the bend term is large and negative; then Newton's "bottom of the parabola" is a top. Gauss-Newton's parabola always opens upward.

The price: the step is exact only when the residual is genuinely a straight line in the parameters (page 3 will show that is "linear regression"). Otherwise the residual's tangent is only right nearby, and Gauss-Newton takes several steps — page 2 — and can overshoot — page 4.

Step 4 — the same formula in matrix clothes

Before matrices: what if two errors want different movements?

Use one weight and two targets, 1 and 3: r₁(w) = w − 1 and r₂(w) = w − 3. At w = 0, the residuals are −1 and −3, both with slope 1. Their tangent predictions after a movement Δ are Δ − 1 and Δ − 3.

local loss q(Δ) = (Δ − 1)² + (Δ − 3)²its derivative = 2(Δ − 1) + 2(Δ − 3)set it to zero: 4Δ − 8 = 0 ⇒ Δ = 2

Now shorten that same arithmetic: r = [−1, −3], J = [[1], [1]], so JᵀJ = 2 and Jᵀr = −4. The matrix equation below becomes 2Δ = 4. It is still minimizing the squared tangent errors.

ObjectShapeMeaning
rn × 1One current error per residual
Jn × POne residual per row; one parameter per column
ΔP × 1One proposed movement per parameter
r + JΔn × 1All predicted residuals after the move
Jᵀ(r + JΔ)P × 1Half the local loss gradient; set this to zero

With P parameters and n residuals: r is a vector of n numbers; J is an n×P table, row i = the slopes of residual i with respect to each parameter. Everything above goes through with transposes in the right places:

gradient: g = 2·Jᵀr # P numbers curvature: HGN = 2·JᵀJ # P×P, built from first derivatives only step: (2JᵀJ)·Δ = −2Jᵀr ⇒ (JᵀJ)·Δ = −Jᵀr # the 2 cancels, as before

Textbooks write the loss as ½·Σr² precisely so that the 2 never appears: the gradient becomes Jᵀr, the curvature JᵀJ, the step identical. This course keeps L = r² because chapters 1–7 did, and prints the cancellation instead.

JᵀJ is still P×P — the size problem has not gone away, only the second-derivative problem has. Page 3 solves a 2×2 by hand; page 5 is how networks avoid forming it at all.

Refresher: reading JᵀJ and Jᵀr as tables, on a 2×2

Two residuals, two parameters: J is 2×2. Row i is residual i's two slopes. JᵀJ[a,b] = sum over residuals of (slope wrt a)·(slope wrt b): the diagonal collects each parameter's squared slopes, the off-diagonal collects how often the two parameters move a residual together. Jᵀr[a] = sum over residuals of (slope wrt a)·(residual) = half the gradient of parameter a. Page 3 computes both with the actual numbers 10, 4, 4, 2 and −17, −5.

A two-layer model: Gauss-Newton in four steps, Newton uphill

The smallest model whose residual is not a line

Stack two layers with one weight each and share the weight: y = w·(w·x) = w²·x. Same data x = 3, t = 10. Now:

residual r(w) = 3w² − 10 # a parabola in w, not a line slope J(w) = 6w # changes with w bend r″ = 6 # no longer zero: the bend term is alive best w: 3w² = 10 ⇒ w = √(10/3) = 1.8257 # (also −1.8257; and w = 0 is a hilltop: r = −10, L = 100)

This is the honest reason a neural network's loss is not a parabola in its weights: even with linear layers, weights multiply each other, so the output bends as a function of the parameters.

The tangent’s zero is a prediction. Check it, then redraw.

The repeated operation is measure r and J here → solve for Δ → evaluate the real model at w + Δ → repeat. Setting the tangent to zero does not make the true residual zero unless the tangent is exact.

Look at the distance to 1.8257 after each step: 0.83 → 0.34 → 0.027 → 0.00016. Each step roughly squares the error once the tangent is good. That is quadratic convergence in this smooth example with a zero-residual solution and nonzero J. It is a local result, not a guarantee for every nonlinear least-squares problem; a nonzero-residual optimum can have slower convergence.

From loss 49 to loss 9604. Nothing is broken: Newton solves "where is the gradient zero" using the local curvature, and at w = 1 the loss curves downward (it is on the slope between the hilltop at 0 and the valley at 1.83). The fitted local parabola with curvature −12 opens down; its stationary point is that model’s peak, at w = −6. The actual loss is not stationary there. Gauss-Newton's parabola is the residual's tangent squared: it opens up, no matter where you stand.

Read the picture top-down: the tangent at w = 1 (green, top) crosses zero at 2.167; square that tangent and you get the green parabola at the bottom, whose floor is exactly 0 at 2.167 — Gauss-Newton's step. Newton's red parabola matches the loss at w = 1 in value, slope and curvature, and precisely because the curvature there is negative, it has no floor.

In one dimension, a learning rate is a guess at 1/curvature, and the best guess is as good as the real thing: 0.005 ≈ 1.2/240. What you paid for it was a five-value sweep, one value of which never converges — and the guess is only right near the solution, where the curvature happens to be 240. Gauss-Newton recomputed the curvature at every row of its table: 72, 338, 247, 240. The real payoff is not one dimension; it is the next page, where no single number can be right for two directions at once.

Same 1.8257. The reported Jacobian and function evaluation counts describe solver work, not a one-to-one count of accepted steps. method='lm' adds safeguards; its evaluation counts cannot alone explain the difference from the four hand-computed Gauss-Newton updates.

Two parameters: the tilt a per-parameter number cannot fix

Model y = w·x + b, two data points

Point A is the course's (x = 3, t = 10). Point B is the one in your practice/optimizers.py: (x = 1, t = 1). Start where that file starts, (w, b) = (1, 1).

pred A = 1·3 + 1 = 4 r₁ = 4 − 10 = −6 pred B = 1·1 + 1 = 2 r₂ = 2 − 1 = 1 L = 36 + 1 = 37

One step, exact, because both residuals are straight lines in (w, b): the tangents are the residuals. Two unknowns, two tangents, one linear system — that is Gauss-Newton with as many residuals as parameters.

The same step through the official formula

Page 1 ended with (JᵀJ)·Δ = −Jᵀr. Build both sides from the rows above:

The off-diagonal is a concrete cross-effect

Here JᵀJ = [[10, 4], [4, 2]]. The full Hessian for this course’s loss is twice that: H = [[20, 8], [8, 4]]. Increase w by 0.1 without changing b: gw changes by 2, and gb changes by 0.8. That second change is the coupling.

At the start, g = (−34, −10), so an update of the form −positive_scale × g increases both coordinates. The exact move is (+3.5, −4.5): b must decrease while w increases. The coupled solve can produce that move; independent positive scaling of the current gradient cannot.

Refresher: does a diagonal optimizer move only along one axis at a time?

No. SGD and Adam update all coordinates together. “Diagonal” means the scaling has no explicit cross-parameter entries; it does not mean alternating horizontal and vertical moves.

Same answer, more arithmetic. Why the detour exists: with more residuals than parameters, the tangent equations may be inconsistent. Least squares still has a solution, characterized by JᵀJΔ = −Jᵀr. That solution is unique when J has full column rank; a singular matrix may give many solutions. Bottom of this page.

Adam keeps one number per parameter. It applies a diagonal scaling to its stored gradient trend. Positive axis scalings do not remove this bowl’s cross-term. At the initial point, positive independent scaling of the current negative gradient cannot produce the exact step (3.5, −4.5), because it would increase both parameters. The coupled solve accounts for their joint effect.

More residuals than parameters: the normal equations

Add a third point (x = 2, t = 4). At (1, 1): pred = 3, r₃ = −1, slopes (2, 1). Three tangents, two unknowns:

3Δw + Δb = 6 Δw + Δb = −1 2Δw + Δb = 1 the first two give (3.5, −4.5); plug into the third: 7 − 4.5 = 2.5 ≠ 1. # no Δ zeroes all three

So pick the Δ that makes the tangents as small as possible in the squared sense: minimize (tangent₁)² + (tangent₂)² + (tangent₃)². Setting that sum's derivative to zero gives exactly (JᵀJ)Δ = −Jᵀr — that is what the transposed form was for.

J = [[3,1],[1,1],[2,1]] r = (−6, 1, −1) JᵀJ = [[9+1+4, 3+1+2], = [[14, 6], Jᵀr = [3·(−6) + 1 + 2·(−1), = [−19, [3+1+2, 1+1+1]] [ 6, 3]] −6 + 1 − 1] −6] 14Δw + 6Δb = 19 6Δw + 3Δb = 6 ⇒ Δb = 2 − 2Δw ⇒ 14Δw + 12 − 12Δw = 19 ⇒ Δw = 3.5, Δb = −5 new (w, b) = (4.5, −4) residuals (−0.5, −0.5, 1) L = 0.25 + 0.25 + 1 = 1.5
output — real run, numpy 2.0.2 np.linalg.lstsq(X, t)[0] = [ 4.5 -4. ] # the closed-form least-squares line: same point

At the optimum, Jᵀr = 0, not necessarily r = 0. Check the residual order used above, (−0.5, −0.5, +1): 3(−0.5) + 1(−0.5) + 2(+1) = 0 and −0.5 − 0.5 + 1 = 0. Neither parameter has a remaining first-order direction of improvement.

Refresher: should I form (JᵀJ)⁻¹ in code?

The inverse is notation for the solution when it exists. In code, solve the least-squares problem with a suitable factorization; QR or SVD avoids explicitly forming the inverse and can handle rank deficiency. “The normal equations have a solution” does not imply that the solution is unique or that their matrix is safely invertible.

1.5 is the best any straight line can do on those three points, and Gauss-Newton found it in one step. Recognize the formula: for a full-column-rank linear model, the step is Δ = −(JᵀJ)&supmin;¹Jᵀr. Add that movement to the current parameters to recover the least-squares solution, written in closed form as (XᵀX)&supmin;¹Xᵀt of every statistics course. Gauss-Newton on a nonlinear model is "solve linear regression on the tangent, repeat."

No learning rate means no brakes — Levenberg–Marquardt

A bad start on the two-layer model

Same r = 3w² − 10, but start at w = 0.3, near the hilltop:

r = 3·0.09 − 10 = −9.73 J = 6·0.3 = 1.8 L = 94.67 Gauss-Newton: Δ = −r/J = 9.73/1.8 = 5.406 ⇒ w = 5.706, r = 3·32.55 − 10 = 87.66, L = 7684

Loss up 81× in one step. The tangent at 0.3 is nearly flat (J = 1.8), so its zero is far away, and the tangent was only ever trustworthy nearby. Nothing in "−r/J" knows the word nearby. (On this toy it recovers — 5.71 → 3.14 → 2.10 → 1.84 → 1.826 — because the far side of the valley is still a valley. A million-parameter model is not owed that luck.)

Newton from 0.3 is worse in a different way: H = 2·1.8² + 2·(−9.73)·6 = 6.48 − 116.76 = −110.3, step −0.318, w = −0.018 — then it converges to w = 0 and stays: loss 100, gradient zero, the hilltop. Newton found a stationary point, as asked.

Damping: charge for distance

Gauss-Newton minimizes the squared tangent. Add a price on the step length and minimize both:

(1) f(Δ) = (r + JΔ)² + λ·Δ² # λ ≥ 0: the price per unit of squared step (2) slope of f: 2J(r + JΔ) + 2λΔ = 0 (3) divide by 2, collect Δ: (J² + λ)·Δ = −J·r (4) Δ = −J·r / (J² + λ) matrix form: (JᵀJ + λI)·Δ = −Jᵀr

The penalty acts on movement, not on the size of the weight

With the current weight frozen at 0.3, draw the local objective as a function of Δ. Adding λΔ² lifts the far-away part of that local objective and moves its minimum closer to Δ = 0.

Refresher: is λΔ² the same as weight decay λw²?

No. This penalty discourages a long move from the current point. It does not prefer weights near zero. If the current weight were 100, Δ = 0 would still incur no movement penalty.

Two ends of one dial:

  • λ = 0: the denominator is J² — plain Gauss-Newton.
  • λ ≫ J²: the denominator is ≈ λ, so Δ ≈ −J·r/λ = −g/(2λ) — plain gradient descent with learning rate 1/(2λ).

The learning rate page 0 abandoned comes back as a safety dial. Big λ: shorter, gradient-shaped trial steps; safety still comes from checking the true loss. Small λ: long, curvature-shaped steps. This simple teaching variant of Levenberg–Marquardt turns the dial as follows (practical solvers often use the ratio of actual to predicted reduction):

try the step. loss fell → accept it, λ ← λ/10 # the tangent was trustworthy: trust it more loss rose → reject it, λ ← 10λ, try again from the same w

λ's other job: your practice/optimizers.py model, where the equations don't pick a step

That file fits y = w·x + b to ONE point (x = 1, t = 1) from (w, b) = (1, 1). Do Gauss-Newton on it:

r = 1 + 1 − 1 = 1 J = [1, 1] (one row: slope wrt w is x = 1, slope wrt b is 1) JᵀJ = [[1, 1],[1, 1]] determinant = 1·1 − 1·1 = 0 # singular: no inverse, no unique Δ why: one tangent equation, Δw + Δb = −1, two unknowns. Every (w, b) with w + b = 1 fits the point exactly.

A movement (a, −a) leaves the prediction w + b unchanged. The data therefore cannot distinguish that direction: J(a, −a) = 0. That is what “singular” means here. Damping supplies a preference for shorter movement; a minimum-norm least-squares solution can also select the shortest step.

Refresher: can damping move us if J is exactly zero?

Not by itself. On r = 3w² − 10 at w = 0, J = 0 and g = 0 even though the loss is 100. Plain −r/J is undefined; with positive λ, −Jr/(J² + λ) = 0. Damping makes the solve well defined, but does not manufacture a direction away from an exact stationary point.

Two parameters, one data point, a whole line of perfect answers. Plain Gauss-Newton has no way to choose. Add λ:

(JᵀJ + λI)Δ = −Jᵀr: (1+λ)Δw + Δb = −1 Δw + (1+λ)Δb = −1 symmetric in w and b, so Δw = Δb = d: (2 + λ)·d = −1 ⇒ d = −1/(2 + λ) λ = 1: d = −0.333 → (0.667, 0.667) λ = 0.1: d = −0.476 → (0.524, 0.524) λ → 0: d = −0.5 → (0.5, 0.5)

As λ → 0 the step goes to (−0.5, −0.5): of all the points on the line w + b = 1, the one closest to where you stand. That is what the λΔ² price buys when the equations are indifferent, and it is one reason damped solvers are useful. Gauss-Newton can also use a line search, other trust-region strategies, or a minimum-norm solve. Now the Adam loop in that file (lr = 1e-3, run as written):

output — real run of practice/optimizers.py's loop (selected epochs) epoch 0: loss=1.000000, w=0.9997, b=0.9997 epoch 99: loss=0.660715, w=0.9054, b=0.9054 epoch 499: loss=0.000401, w=0.5092, b=0.5092 epoch 999: loss=0.000000, w=0.5000, b=0.5000

Same destination (0.5, 0.5) — w and b get identical gradients since x = 1, so Adam also walks the diagonal — first within 0.001 of a perfect fit at epoch 512. The minimum-norm Gauss-Newton step (−0.5, −0.5) reaches that fit exactly; a finite damped step does not. At λ = 1 it reaches (2/3, 2/3), with residual 1/3. The comparison is an illustration of this tiny linear problem, not a general speed ratio.

Neural networks: linearize the network, keep the loss

Step 1 — what gets linearized when the loss is not a square

So far the residual was "prediction minus target" and the loss was its square. A network outputs a vector f(w) (logits), and the loss ℓ(f) is whatever you chose — squared error was the special case ℓ(f) = (f − t)². The recipe generalizes with one change of wording: linearize the network, keep the loss exactly.

network tangent: f(w + Δ) ≈ f + J·Δ # J = ∂f/∂w, one row per output loss of the tangent: ℓ(f + JΔ) its curvature in Δ: HGGN = Jᵀ · (∂²ℓ/∂f²) · J # the loss's own curvature, sandwiched by J check, squared error: ∂²ℓ/∂f² = 2 ⇒ H = 2JᵀJ ✓ (page 1)

Read the sandwich from right to left

Call the loss’s curvature in output space B = ∂²ℓ/∂f². For any proposed parameter direction v, the product JᵀBJv has three stages:

OperationSpace you are inWhat it means
vP parameter coordinatesA direction in which weights could move
JvOutput coordinatesHow that direction changes the outputs
B(Jv)Output coordinatesWeight that output change by the loss’s curvature
Jᵀ[B(Jv)]P parameter coordinatesBring the curvature effect back to the weights

The GGN step solves (JᵀBJ)Δ = −g, usually with damping. For squared error, B = 2I and this reduces to the equation you already solved. For cross-entropy, the loss of the linearized network is not generally a quadratic; GGN uses its curvature at Δ = 0 to form a local quadratic step.

Refresher: can I see the sandwich with actual numbers?

Let two logits be (3w + b, w + b), so J = [[3, 1], [1, 1]]. At a point with probabilities p = (0.7, 0.3), B = [[0.21, −0.21], [−0.21, 0.21]]. A direction v = (1, 0) passes through the three stages:

Jv = (3, 1)B(Jv) = (0.21×3 − 0.21×1, −0.21×3 + 0.21×1) = (0.42, −0.42)JᵀB(Jv) = (3×0.42 − 0.42, 0.42 − 0.42) = (0.84, 0)

Now try v = (0, 1), changing only b. Both logits change equally: Jv = (1, 1). Then B(Jv) = (0, 0). Adding the same number to both logits leaves softmax probabilities unchanged, so this direction has zero curvature. Positive semidefinite means “never negative,” not “every direction is strictly positive.”

This is the Generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN). Same sales pitch as page 1: J comes from backprop; the middle factor is a small matrix (outputs × outputs) you can write down; and if the loss is convex in f — squared error and cross-entropy both are — the sandwich can never be negative.

For softmax cross-entropy the middle factor is diag(p) − p·pᵀ, p = the predicted probabilities. For this softmax likelihood and logit parameterization, with matching input averaging, HGGN equals the model’s Fisher information matrix. Solving with that matrix yields a natural-gradient direction; damping and step selection remain separate choices.

Refresher: the cross-entropy middle factor on two classes

p = (0.7, 0.3): diag(p) − ppᵀ = [[0.7 − 0.49, −0.21], [−0.21, 0.3 − 0.09]] = [[0.21, −0.21], [−0.21, 0.21]]. Its rows sum to zero (shifting all logits equally changes nothing), and it is largest when p is uncertain: at p = (0.5, 0.5) the diagonal is 0.25, at p = (0.99, 0.01) it is 0.0099. A very confident softmax prediction has small output-space curvature. Its loss gradient can still be large if the confident prediction is wrong: the gradient is p minus the target vector.

Step 2 — why nobody forms it

You can apply a matrix without storing the matrix

Reuse J = [[3, 1], [1, 1]] from the two-point example. Try a probe direction v = (1, 0):

v = (1, 0) # one unit in the w directionJv = (3, 1) # predicted changes to the two residualsJᵀ(Jv) = (3×3 + 1×1, 1×3 + 1×1) = (10, 4)with λ = 1: Jᵀ(Jv) + λv = (11, 4)

Those numbers match multiplying by [[10, 4], [4, 2]], then adding v. In a network, automatic differentiation can compute the forward product Jv and the reverse product Jᵀu without writing out J. An iterative linear solver asks for such products repeatedly to find Δ. Ordinary backward() gives a particular weighted sum of Jacobian rows, not the entire Jacobian as a free stored table.

  • J is not one backward pass. The gradient of the scalar loss is one backward pass. J has one row per output per example: a batch of B examples with C classes would naively need one reverse-mode row computation per output, B·C rows. Vectorization, forward mode, or direct products can change that cost; one ordinary scalar-loss backward pass does not materialize J.
  • JᵀJ is P×P. 7B parameters → 4.9×10¹&sup9; entries. Even the diagonal alone is a second copy of the model.
  • Minibatch noise. A curvature estimated from one batch of a few hundred sequences is noisy; a noisy curvature in a denominator produces wild steps, so λ has to do heavy work, and a heavily damped Gauss-Newton is just gradient descent at a high price.

"Adam is a diagonal Gauss-Newton" — a fine sentence with two asterisks

There are two different averages hiding in that analogy

Two per-example gradientsCompute itResult
Square the batch-average gradient((+2 − 2)/2)²0
Average the per-example squared gradients(2² + (−2)²)/24

Adam normally accumulates the square of the batch gradient. The empirical Fisher uses averages of per-example outer products. Even its diagonal need not equal Adam’s v. Both are different from the model-label expectation used by the true Fisher.

Refresher: when does GGN equal Fisher, and does confidence imply a small gradient?

For a softmax categorical model with cross-entropy in its logits, the output-space matrix is B = diag(p) − ppᵀ. Pulling it back through the logit Jacobian gives the model’s Fisher, with the same input averaging. This equality is not a statement that all losses, all empirical gradient squares, and all damped updates are interchangeable.

Confidence is separate from correctness. If p = (0.99, 0.01) but the target is class 2, B has small diagonal entries 0.0099 while the logit gradient p − target = (0.99, −0.99) is large. Small curvature does not force a small gradient.

  • Empirical vs. true. Adam squares the gradient of the loss against the true labels. The GGN / Fisher uses the model's own predicted labels (that is what Sophia's estimator does deliberately). The empirical version can be a poor curvature proxy — at a perfect fit its diagonal is zero while the true curvature is not (Kunstner et al. 2019).
  • The square root. Gauss-Newton divides by the curvature. Adam divides by its square root, for the reason chapter 3 gave (normalize the gradient's scale, not the loss's curvature). Halfway between SGD and Gauss-Newton in how hard it corrects.

Where Gauss-Newton proper is the day job: curve fitting (scipy's least_squares), camera calibration and bundle adjustment in SLAM (Ceres, g2o — Levenberg–Marquardt on a sparse JᵀJ with millions of parameters, which works because each residual touches only a few of them).

Check yourself: why is “set the residual tangent to zero” not the whole matrix story?

Different residuals may ask for incompatible movements. Then we minimize their squared sum. The resulting predicted residuals can be nonzero even when their combined loss gradient is zero.

Check yourself: what was exact in the first picture?

For L = (3w − 10)², the gradient is a line and the residual is also a line. Both tangents are exact everywhere, so both methods find the bottom in one step. With a curved residual, only a local approximation is being solved.

Traps

  • "Gauss-Newton approximates the Hessian" is true but backwards as a picture. It linearizes the model; the JᵀJ Hessian is what falls out. Say the picture first.
  • Dropping the bend term is not an accuracy loss you pay for speed — it also buys a curvature that is never negative. Newton can point uphill (w = 1 → −6). When g is nonzero, an exact Gauss-Newton solve gives a downhill direction to first order, but its full step can overshoot and increase the actual loss, as page 4 demonstrates.
  • "No learning rate" is not "no hyperparameter": λ is one, but it tunes itself from a loss check, which a fixed lr cannot.
  • Singular JᵀJ means the linearized residuals do not determine every parameter direction. Damping gives a unique shorter trial step; its limit as λ approaches zero selects the minimum-norm least-squares step. Finite damping does not generally reach an exact fit.
  • In one dimension a swept SGD matches Gauss-Newton. The win is multi-dimensional and it is the off-diagonal — the tilt.

Follow-up questions, one-line answers

questionone-line answer
Why is it allowed to drop the second-derivative term?It is r·r″: it needs second derivatives, it vanishes as the residual goes to zero, and it is the only term that can make the curvature negative.
When is Gauss-Newton exact?When the residual is linear in the parameters — linear regression: the single step is the normal equations, (XᵀX)&supmin;¹Xᵀt.
Gauss-Newton vs Newton on a nonlinear model?They agree as the omitted bend term vanishes, for example near a regular zero-residual solution. Gauss-Newton’s squared tangent model is nonnegative; a full step still needs a check on the actual loss.
What does Levenberg–Marquardt add?λI on the diagonal: λ = 0 is Gauss-Newton, λ → ∞ is gradient descent with lr = 1/(2λ); λ is divided by 10 after a good step and multiplied by 10 after a bad one.
Why isn't it used to train LLMs?Explicit Jacobians and P×P curvature matrices are expensive, and estimates can be noisy. Structured approximations and matrix-vector products reduce that cost; one ordinary backward pass does not return the whole Jacobian.
Relation to natural gradient?With cross-entropy, the GGN matrix Jᵀ(diag(p) − ppᵀ)J is the Fisher information; the GGN step is the natural-gradient step.
Relation to Adam?Adam uses a history of squared batch gradients with a square-root divisor. This is not an exact diagonal GGN or empirical-Fisher calculation, and it does not perform a coupled curvature solve.
How is convergence different?Quadratic locally in this regular zero-residual example (0.34 → 0.027 → 0.00016). Nonzero-residual problems need not have that rate.
