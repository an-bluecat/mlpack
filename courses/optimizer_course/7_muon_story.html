A mixer with four knobs, and one simple job

The story starts with a prediction

Imagine a tiny two-channel audio mixer. Two numbers come in: the left signal and the right signal. Two numbers come out. For this exercise, its job is simply to copy the inputs: left stays left, right stays right.

It has four adjustable knobs. Each knob says how much of one input to send to one output. Put those four weights in a table and call the table W.

From input 1From input 2
To output 16.54.5
To output 24.56.5

These are deliberately bad starting weights. A row tells you how to make one output:

output 1 = 6.5 × input 1 + 4.5 × input 2output 2 = 4.5 × input 1 + 6.5 × input 2

Writing y = W @ x is just a shorter way to write those two lines. x is the input pair, y is the prediction pair, and @ means matrix multiplication: multiply along a row and add.

Try two test sounds

TestInput xWanted output tActual prediction y
Left only[1, 0][1, 0][6.5, 4.5]
Right only[0, 1][0, 1][4.5, 6.5]

For the left-only test, the first output is 6.5 × 1 + 4.5 × 0 = 6.5. The second is 4.5 × 1 + 6.5 × 0 = 4.5. The mixer makes the sound too loud and leaks it into the other channel.

The inputs and targets are given data. Training changes the four weights in W. A perfect mixer would have weights [[1, 0], [0, 1]]. That special “copy unchanged” matrix is called the identity, written I.

What we are going to discover

First measure the mistakes. Then ask how changing a weight would change them. That produces a gradient matrix. We will inspect what that matrix does to combinations of inputs, before introducing singular values or Newton–Schulz.

This small task makes the arithmetic visible. It is a teaching example, not a benchmark for choosing an optimizer.

Pause: Which numbers are allowed to change: the input, the target, or W?

W. The optimizer adjusts the four weights; the two training examples stay fixed.

Where does the gradient matrix actually come from?

First subtract the target

An error is prediction minus target. Keep its sign: positive means too high; negative means too low. Only after doing that subtraction will we give the error a short name, e.

TestPrediction − targetError e
Left only[6.5 − 1, 4.5 − 0][5.5, 4.5]
Right only[4.5 − 0, 6.5 − 1][4.5, 5.5]

Square the four errors and add them to get a single score. Here we use half that sum so the derivative of each term is just its error. This lesson’s convention is L = ½ × sum of squared errors.

L = ½ × (5.5² + 4.5² + 4.5² + 5.5²) = 50.5

The factor ½ changes the numerical gradient scale, not which weights fit the data best.

Follow just one knob before doing all four

Take the top-left weight, W[0,0]. The zeros are row and column numbers, counted from zero. On the left-only test, increasing this knob by a tiny amount increases output 1 by the same amount, because input 1 is 1.

The loss’s slope with respect to that output is its error, 5.5. Therefore the loss’s slope with respect to this knob is 5.5 × 1 = 5.5. On the right-only test, input 1 is zero, so this knob contributes 4.5 × 0 = 0.

gradient for W[0,0] = 5.5 × 1 + 4.5 × 0 = 5.5

That is the chain rule in words: how fast the loss changes with the output times how fast the output changes with this weight. Add the contributions because our loss adds the two tests.

Now do the same calculation for every knob

W[0,0]5.5 × 14.5 × 05.5
W[0,1]5.5 × 04.5 × 14.5
W[1,0]4.5 × 15.5 × 04.5

W stores the current knobs. G stores the loss slopes for those knobs. They have the same shape, but they are different objects. Subtracting a small positive multiple of G moves downhill here.

Pause: Why is G[0,1] 4.5 rather than the prediction 6.5?

This knob controls input 2 → output 1. Only the right-only test activates that connection. Its output-1 error is 4.5 − 0 = 4.5.

Four similar-looking numbers hide a 10-to-1 difference

Why look at a gradient as a function?

Suppose we change the weights using ordinary gradient descent: W_new = W − lr × G. The learning rate lr controls how far we move.

For any fixed input x, that weight change produces this output change:

old prediction = W @ xnew prediction = (W − lr × G) @ xchange in prediction = −lr × (G @ x)

So G @ x tells us how strongly the proposed update changes the mixer’s response to input x, before the minus sign and learning rate. That is why treating G as a matrix matters.

Listen to two combinations of the inputs

One pattern raises both inputs together: [1, 1]. Another raises one and lowers the other: [1, −1]. A direction means a pattern of changes; it need not be one input by itself.

G @ [1, 1] = [5.5 + 4.5, 4.5 + 5.5] = [10, 10] = 10 × [1, 1]G @ [1, −1] = [5.5 − 4.5, 4.5 − 5.5] = [ 1, −1] = 1 × [1, −1]

The update acts 10 times as strongly on “together” as on “opposite.” Yet the four entries, 5.5 and 4.5, look quite similar. Comparing individual entries misses that cancellation in the second pattern.

Give the two arrows names

q₊ = [1, 1] / √2 names the unit-length together direction. q₋ = [1, −1] / √2 names the unit-length opposite direction.

The subscripts + and − are labels for the patterns. A unit vector has length 1; √2 appears because 1² + 1² = 2.

G @ q₊ = 10 q₊G @ q₋ = 1 q₋

Now the output lengths are exactly 10 and 1. Later we will call those lengths singular values. First, remember what we measured.

Pause: Are 10 and 1 two entries of G?

No. They are gains: how much G stretches two unit-length input patterns. We found them by multiplying the whole matrix by each pattern.

Keep the patterns; give them comparable step sizes

One volume knob cannot fix the imbalance

Dividing the whole gradient by 10 reduces its strongest gain to 1, but the other gain becomes 0.1. A single learning rate scales both again; their ratio remains 10.

Muon’s idealized transformation asks for something different: keep the input-to-output direction pairs, but change each nonzero gain to 1. In our example that gives O = [[1, 0], [0, 1]], because O must copy both perpendicular patterns at unit strength.

O is a proposed update direction. We will subtract a scaled version from W. We are not replacing the model’s weights with O.

Compare using the same largest permitted output change

Use −0.1 × G/10 for the globally rescaled gradient, and −0.1 × O for the ideal Muon step. Each can change the output by at most 0.1 for a unit-length input. That shared limit makes the comparison precise.

Now the name “singular value” has something to refer to

A general matrix has perpendicular input directions, corresponding perpendicular output directions, and a gain for each pair. The gains are its singular values. SVD is a procedure that finds these directions and gains.

Write the input directions as columns of V, the output directions as columns of U, and the gains on the diagonal of S. Then:

G = U @ S @ V.Tread it right to left:V.T: measure how much of each input pattern is presentS: multiply those amounts by their gainsU: assemble the corresponding output patterns

.T swaps rows and columns. Here both U and V equal Q = [[1, 1], [1, −1]] / √2, and S = diag(10, 1). “diag” puts its numbers on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

G = Q @ diag(10, 1) @ Q.TO = Q @ diag( 1, 1) @ Q.T = I

That replacement is called orthogonalization for our full-rank square example: O preserves lengths and perpendicularity. It changes the update’s gains, while retaining its direction pairs.

Pause: Why not divide every entry by its own magnitude?

All four entries of G are positive, so that produces [[1, 1], [1, 1]]. Applied to [1, −1], it gives [0, 0]: the opposite pattern disappears! This is also the idealized first Adam step’s sign pattern when epsilon is ignored, not a claim about every later Adam step.

The next batch may disagree. Keep a memory.

The gradient we just measured is today’s report

A real training run sees different data over time. Its gradient changes from batch to batch. Momentum keeps some of the previous report instead of relying entirely on the newest one.

Create another 2 × 2 table called M. It starts at zero. For this teaching version, keep 90% of the old M and add 10% of today’s gradient G:

M_new = 0.9 × M_old + 0.1 × Gfirst report: M_old = [[0, 0], [0, 0]]so M_new = 0.1 × G = [[0.55, 0.45], [0.45, 0.55]]

The number 0.9 is a chosen momentum setting, often called β (beta). This averaging rule is an exponential moving average. All four entries get the same rule, but each keeps its own history.

What does “remember” mean in numbers?

Isolate one entry for a moment. Suppose its successive reported gradients are +2, then −2. This is a small memory demonstration, separate from the mixer’s training run.

first: M = 0.9 × 0 + 0.1 × 2 = 0.20second: M = 0.9 × 0.2 + 0.1 × (−2) = −0.02

The new report almost cancels the old one. If the second report were +2 instead, M would become 0.9 × 0.2 + 0.1 × 2 = 0.38. Repeating signals accumulate; reversals can cancel. This does not prove every surviving signal is useful.

The order matters

The Muon story is now: measure G → update the memory M → reshape M’s directional gains → move W. Orthogonalization operates on the remembered update, not on the weights.

On our very first step, M is just 0.1 × G. Its gains are 1 and 0.1 instead of 10 and 1. The ratio and direction pairs are unchanged. On later steps M mixes multiple gradients, so it need not point where the latest G points.

A deliberate simplification

We use plain EMA momentum to keep the trace short. Practical recipes often use Nesterov momentum, which mixes the current gradient with the buffer before orthogonalization. It changes the input to that operation, not the basic story. The runnable example later states exactly which variant it uses.

Pause: Should we replace W with the orthogonalized matrix?

No. Reshape the momentum into O, then subtract a learning-rate-scaled O from W. W itself does not have to become orthogonal.

How can we approach equal gains without finding an SVD?

First put the numbers on a manageable scale

An SVD tells us the exact direction pairs and gains. The practical update instead uses a small fixed sequence of multiplications. Before seeing the matrix version, watch what it does to one gain at a time.

Start from the first memory matrix, M = [[0.55, 0.45], [0.45, 0.55]]. Its overall size is the square root of the sum of its squared entries, called the Frobenius norm:

size = √(0.55² + 0.45² + 0.45² + 0.55²) = √1.01 ≈ 1.005X = M / size = [[0.5473, 0.4478], [0.4478, 0.5473]]

X is a temporary working copy, not the training inputs x from earlier. Dividing M by this size divides both its gains by the same number:

together gain: 1 / √1.01 = 0.9950opposite gain: 0.1 / √1.01 = 0.0995

The norm is at least as large as any singular value, so the normalized gains lie between 0 and 1. Their ratio is still 10. This step controls the scale; it has not balanced the directions yet.

Now try one number through the recipe

Let s mean one current gain. The original five-round Muon recipe uses this polynomial:

new gain = 3.4445 × s − 4.7750 × s³ + 2.0315 × s⁵

s³ means s × s × s, and s⁵ multiplies five copies. The three coefficients are chosen constants from the published recipe; they are not derived from our mixer. We can test exactly what they do.

small gain: 0.0995 → 0.3381large gain: 0.9950 → 0.7047

The small gain grows, and the large one shrinks. Close to zero, the cubic and fifth-power terms are tiny, so the recipe acts roughly like multiplying by 3.4445.

Also, zero stays zero. An extremely tiny positive gain may remain small after only five rounds. This finite recipe does not guarantee unit gain in every direction.

Pause: Is the first normalization itself the Muon transformation?

No. One global division preserves every gain ratio. The repeated polynomial changes the gains by different relative amounts.

Three matrix multiplications per round, then move the knobs

Start with one entry of a matrix product

The last page explained a recipe for one gain. We need a way to apply it to a whole matrix without explicitly finding those gains.

Take the working matrix X = [[0.5473, 0.4478], [0.4478, 0.5473]]. First form A = X @ X.T. A compares each row of X with each row: multiply matching entries, then add.

A[0,0] = 0.5473² + 0.4478² ≈ 0.5000A[0,1] = 0.5473 × 0.4478 + 0.4478 × 0.5473 ≈ 0.4901A ≈ [[0.5000, 0.4901], [0.4901, 0.5000]]

A is a temporary calculation, not another learned weight table. The symbol @ always means a matrix product; multiplying by an ordinary number scales each entry.

Assemble one round, one line at a time

A = X @ X.TB = −4.7750 × A + 2.0315 × (A @ A)X_new = 3.4445 × X + B @ X

B is just a name for the intermediate table on the second line. Expanding the last line shows three terms:

X_new = 3.4445 X − 4.7750 (X X.T) X + 2.0315 (X X.T)(X X.T) X

Why this arrangement? Look along one input/output direction pair with gain s. X X.T supplies a factor of s² on its output direction. Multiplying by the final X supplies one more s. The three terms therefore have gains s, s³, and s⁵. This is precisely the scalar recipe from the previous page.

The same operation processes every direction pair simultaneously. It is called a Newton–Schulz iteration. Here that name refers to a matrix computation; it is not taking a Newton step on the training loss.

after the first round, X ≈[[0.5214, 0.1833], [0.1833, 0.5214]]

Its two gains are 0.7047 and 0.3381, matching our scalar calculation. Count the products: X @ X.T, A @ A, and B @ X. Five rounds mean 15 matrix multiplications in this formulation, not five.

Now finish the first training step

After five rounds, call the final working matrix O. These values were computed from the unrounded matrices:

[0.7053 -0.0033][-0.0033 0.7053]

Choose learning rate 0.1. The proposed movement is −0.1 × O; the new weight matrix is W − 0.1 × O.

top-left: 6.5 − 0.1 × 0.7053485 = 6.4294651top-right: 4.5 − 0.1 × (−0.0033378) = 4.5003338

The two remaining entries follow by symmetry. The off-diagonal weight increases a little because its proposed update entry is negative. Balancing matrix directions need not preserve each individual entry’s sign.

[6.4295 4.5003][4.5003 6.4295]

Run the mixer again with these new weights. Its loss falls from 50.500000 to 49.732096. That is one complete step: predictions → errors → G → M → O → new W → new predictions.

The next steps use fresh gradients

For this copy task, the gradient at any W is W − I. After moving W, recompute it; do not keep applying the original G. Carry M forward as memory.

A small runnable version, after all the pieces

np.eye(2) builds I; its columns are our two training inputs. np.zeros_like(W) makes a zero-filled table shaped like W. errors @ inputs.T adds error × input contributions, the same gradient rule we evaluated by hand on page 1.

The example keeps a history using .copy() so later steps do not overwrite earlier results. **2 squares each error, and np.sum adds them.

Open the complete NumPy example
"""Small NumPy teaching example: EMA momentum + five NS rounds.No Nesterov, weight decay, or shape-dependent update scaling."""import numpy as npdef reshape_update(momentum): size = np.linalg.norm(momentum) # sqrt(sum of squared entries) if size == 0: return np.zeros_like(momentum) X = momentum / size for _ in range(5): A = X @ X.T B = -4.7750 * A + 2.0315 * (A @ A) X = 3.4445 * X + B @ X return Xdef run(steps=3): W = np.array([[6.5, 4.5], [4.5, 6.5]]) inputs = np.eye(2) # columns are our two training inputs targets = inputs.copy() momentum = np.zeros_like(W) history = [] for step in range(steps): predictions = W @ inputs errors = predictions - targets gradient = errors @ inputs.T momentum = 0.9 * momentum + 0.1 * gradient update = reshape_update(momentum) W = W - 0.1 * update new_errors = W @ inputs - targets new_loss = 0.5 * np.sum(new_errors**2) history.append((step + 1, W.copy(), float(new_loss))) return historyif __name__ == '__main__': np.set_printoptions(precision=6, suppress=True) for step, weights, loss in run(): print(f'Step {step}: loss = {loss:.6f}') print(weights)
Pause: What gets recomputed after each weight update, and what gets remembered?

Predictions, errors and G are recomputed. M survives from the previous step. X, A, B and O are temporary working values for the current update.

One memory, one reshaping operation, one weight update

Say it without any acronyms

Our mixer has four knobs. Its mistakes tell us the loss slope for each knob. Together those slopes form a matrix, which changes different input patterns by different amounts.

We remember recent gradients. Then we reshape that remembered matrix so its existing direction pairs have more comparable gains. A short sequence of matrix multiplications approximates that reshaping. Finally, a learning rate turns the result into a movement of the weights.

Muon = momentum, then approximate orthogonalization of the update.

The important limits of this story

This toy does not establish that Muon beats SGD. In fact, ordinary SGD with lr = 1 solves our quadratic copy task in one step: W_new = W − (W − I) = I. We chose this example to expose the matrix operation, not to manufacture a speed win.

A weak direction is not automatically valuable. It can contain useful information or noise. The arithmetic proves how gains change. Whether that helps learning is an empirical question.

Zero gain is different from small gain. The polynomial leaves an exactly missing direction at zero. In a rank-deficient example, do not blindly replace all SVD values by 1: that would invent motion along null directions. Here both original gains are nonzero.

General matrices can be rectangular. There can be different input and output direction vectors. For a full-rank rectangular ideal update, the rows or columns on the smaller side are orthonormal; both products need not be identity matrices.

The numbers 10 and 1 are update gains, not loss curvatures. All four weight curvatures in our copy task are 1. Muon is not dividing these gains into the gradient as an inverse Hessian, and Newton–Schulz is not Gauss–Newton.

From this teaching version to a training recipe

The original Muon recipe targets hidden-layer matrices and uses an auxiliary optimizer such as AdamW for embeddings, output heads, and scalar/vector parameters. Nesterov momentum is a common choice. See the author’s implementation for the actual parameter grouping and update details.

Larger-model recipes also specify weight decay and matrix-dependent update scaling. These choices affect the effective step size; do not copy our teaching lr into a production run. The Moonlight paper’s scaling section explains those additions.

Five questions to check whether the story stuck

Pause: What is a direction, in our mixer?

A combination of inputs. Together, [1,1], and opposite, [1,−1], are two examples. It is not necessarily a row, column, or individual weight.

Pause: What does the singular value 10 mean?

A unit-length input along the together pattern becomes a vector of length 10 under the gradient map G. That measures the proposed update’s effect before its learning rate and minus sign.

Pause: Why does changing only lr fail to change the ratio?

Because it multiplies both gains by the same number. Ten-to-one stays ten-to-one.

Pause: Does Muon make W orthogonal?

No. It approximately orthogonalizes a momentum-derived update. W is then moved by a scaled version of that update.

Pause: Why can five rounds be enough for an approximate update but not an exact one?

The selected polynomial rapidly changes many small gains, but its values oscillate and 1 is not a fixed point. Extremely tiny or zero gains also need special care; five rounds are a practical recipe, not an exactness guarantee.
